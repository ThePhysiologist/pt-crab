Nervous about Feet

This week, we’re getting into the nerves again in one of my favorite areas of the body, feet. We’re wrapping in a book chapter that gives a fantastic overview, another brief take on Morton’s neuroma, and an RCT about tibial nerve mobilization for tarsal tunnel syndrome. Let’s dive right in.

Nerve injuries of the foot and ankle

The Gist - This breakdown is actually from a textbook, which is one of the first times we’ve gone there, presenting a brief overview of the different, common nerve injuries of the foot and ankle. I’m just going to dive right in.
- Morton’s Neuroma - we cover this in detail later, but in brief, “Morton’s neuroma results in metatarsalgia from the entrapment of the third interdigital nerve between the transverse intermetatarsal ligament and fascia.” It’s usually caused by mechanical compression of that nerve and “causes shooting pain into the toes upon compression.” Wider shoes, activity modification, nerve mobilization, and (eventually) surgery are the common techniques to improve it.
- Superficial fibular nerve entrapment - most common in runners in their 20s and 30s. The nerve innervates plantarflexors and everters and is usually damaged from chronic inversion sprains or compartment syndrome. It often doesn’t come with pain but always with positive Tinel’s. Usually treated surgically but could be treated with gait modification, lidocaine injection, strength training, and myofascial release.
- Sural nerve entrapment - Uncommon entrapment that affects runners, usually in a purely sensory nerve that runs from posterior lower leg to base of the 5th met. Entrapment is usually at lateral heel due to direct trauma or ankle injury. Presentation is calf pain that’s worst at night or with activity. Often treated with NSAIDS, Vitamin B6, and maybe even Vitamin C. Gait training and activity modification can help, otherwise there’s decompression surgery with is usually successful for return to sport.
- Tarsal tunnel syndrome - a tunnel bounded by the medial malleolus, posterior talus, and calcaneus, it contains the tendons of the posterior tibialis, flexor digitorum longus, and flexor hallucis longus muscles, posterior tibial artery/vein, and the posterior tibial nerve, so it’s crowded. Nerve compression or entrapment can be caused by many things: “Extrinsic causes include: anatomic and biomechanical abnormalities (tarsal coalition, valgus/varus hindfoot), poorly fitting shoes, post-traumatic or postsurgical scarring, diabetes, and inflammatory diseases. Intrinsic causes include osteophytes, (perineural) fibrosis, tendinopathy, tenosynovitis, hypertrophic retinaculum, and space-occupying or mass effect lesions,” though trauma is the most common cause. Pain can radiate up or down, so it can mimic sciatica, and usually comes with foot intrinsic weakness and diminished plantar sensation. Treatment is foot strength, orthotics, gait retraining, ankle strength, and sometimes immobilization. Topical creams can help as can steroid injections and eventual surgery if required.
- Baxter’s neuropathy - most common in track and field athletes, this is heel pain caused by the inferior calcaneal nerve (AKA Baxter nerve) being compressed or entrapped. Differentiating from plantar fasciitis can be tough, but treatment is largely the same: gel heel cups, night splinting, stretching, gait modification, ice.

Morton’s neuroma from, The Foot

The Gist -I’m pretty confident that this is the first article I’ve ever covered from The Foot, a journal dedicated to the topic that this Crab is dedicated to. This specific article is a case report on Morton’s neuroma because there isn’t too much out there about the conservative care thereof, at least according to the paper. The patient involved had unilateral dorsal foot pain for the last five years without any observed anatomical alterations even when checked out by 3 MRIs, 1 ultrasound, and 1 Xray. The official diagnosis was “highly-suggestive Morton’s Neuroma clinical presentation”.

In PT, she got 6 visits over the course of 9 weeks with a slowly advancing plan of STM, joint mobilizations, and exercises. Here’s the overall approach

Each session started with a 10-minute soft tissue massage for both the triceps surae muscles and the plantar fascia in order to improve vascularization and tone relaxation. Following that, several Grade IV mobilization techniques were performed as described in Chapter 16 of Maitland’s Peripheral Manipulation 4th Edition for the inferior tibiofibular, ankle and subtalar, tarsometatarsal and intermetatarsal joints. Then, active exercises in order to gain ankle dorsiflexion and strengthening of the foot’s intrinsic muscles using elastic resistance bands were instructed.

Over the course of treatment, the patient improved their dorsiflexion by 2.5cm in the knee to wall test, became negative in the Mulder’s click test, gained 20 points on the LEFS and 10 points on the FAAM, and decrease 22 points on the pain catastrophizing scale.

Tell Me More -I’m as annoyed as you are that the exercises aren’t well defined, but I think we can all guess at them especially in context of the first article of this edition. The discussion section of the piece refers to a few other studies that had a similar procedure and were effective using a combination of manual and ther ex, but this was the first that also looked into catastrophization beliefs too.

Obviously, this is a small case study and we need a lot more info before we know what’s what in this area. But, combined with the other papers cited in this one, it indicates that there’s a clear path forward for PT for Morton’s neuroma and treatment doesn’t all have to be surgical.

Treating Tarsal Tunnel

The Gist -Now that we know what causes it, what about some treatment options? This RCT looked into the effect that tibial nerve mobilization combined with an HEP in comparison to just an HEP could have on tibial nerve diameter, pain, the foot functional index, and Tinel’s sign measurement. They had 40 participants split into two groups of 20. Both groups got the same HEP (which we’ll go into later) while the intervention group also received a manual nerve mobilization.

The mobilization had the patient in supine, hip and knee flexed to 45° and ankle in neutral. To do the glide, the toes are put into extension and ankle into dorsiflexion and eversion, then the knee is taken to full extension while the ankle is pushed into plantar flexion and toe flexion. The mobilization was done twice per week for 4 weeks, 5 sets of 10 reps with a 1-minute rest between sets. That’s a lot of mobilization.

The HEP included gastroc, soleus, and plantar fascia stretching. 10 reps 3x/day for four weeks. Each stretch was 20-30 seconds long with a minimum of 10 seconds of rest between.

At the end of all this, 75% of the intervention group had no Tinel’s sign while only 25% of the control group got to this point. Pain in both groups improved, but pain decreased by an average of 1.5 points more in intervention than control. The foot function index scores improved in the intervention group as well, but not in control.

Tell Me More -One challenge to the assessment of the usefulness of this study is pure contact time. The intervention group spent a lot more time with physical therapists than the control group did and that’s definitely a confounder. It would also be difficult to get most patients on board with the level of exercise applied to either group in this study. So this may not be super applicable to your patients unless you can really get them on board.

If you can get them on board, it may well work though. And that’s nice for a condition that is often recalcitrant to PT. You may not need to apply this extreme degree of exercise to get things going, but also, you may, so test it out if this patient lands on your table.

What have we learned today? A summary.

There are a lot of ways to injure a nerve in your foot and/or ankle and many of them need surgery. But not all. The textbook at the beginning is aimed at medicine, so it’s going to default to surgery anyway. For most of those conditions, the textbook just says they should try “conservative management” or “physical therapy” without going any deeper. They don’t know what we do, but we do know how to do it well. For Morton’s neuroma and Tarsal Tunnel Syndrome, there are some useful treatment methods in there. The nerve glide for TTS, fascial mobilization for Morton’s neuroma, and the exercises seem to help. We also know that activity and gait modification can work for many of these conditions, so put on your thinking cap and apply it.
