Issue 176

Peripheral Neuropathy

Believe it or not, PTs can treat this, and pretty effectively. You can’t make the underlying neuropathy better but you can reduce the functional limitations from it. And that’s kind of our thing. Tangentially, PTs can help with diabetes management too. Exercise is one of the best ways to improve diabetes. And that’s kind of our thing too. So let’s learn how to better treat peripheral neuropathy today, shall we?

Sensorimotor and Gait Training

The Gist -We already know that aerobic exercise and Tai Chi can improve nerve function in people with neuropathy and that even just brisk walking can make a difference. This RCT wanted to find out if specific gait training and sensorimotor training could improve proprioception and nerve conduction when compared to diabetes and foot care education alone. The exercise was 3x/wk for 8 weeks. 10 minute warmup, 50-60 minute exercise, 10 minute cool down. Other than walking or cycling for the warmup and cool down, the exercise included “wall slides, bridging exercises, prone plank, sit to stand, wobble board exercises, one leg stance, heel and toe raise, tandem stance gradually progressed to different grades using unstable surface (Thera Band® Stability Trainer) and gait training (different patterns of walking).” With full details in the paper’s appendices.

Before and after the interventions, they tested proprioception on the Pedalo®-Sensamove Balance Test Pro with Miniboard which is a wobble board with visual feedback. Nerve conduction was measured with nerve conduction studies on the deep fibular and tibial nerves and they also used surface EMG to check out the function of tib anterior, medial gastroc, and vastus lateralis.

Proprioception was significantly better, nerve conduction velocity increased by 6% more in the intervention group than control in the fibular nerve and did well in other areas as well that are more complicated to explain. Basically, people in the intervention group improved. So this definitely made a difference.

Tell Me More -Did it make a “life-applicable” difference? That’s unclear. They didn’t use any functional testing as outcome measures, so we don’t know if these people improved on the TUG, or maximal gait speed, or 10m walk test, or anything like that. We do know that real physiological change occurred in these people in ways that should be clinically meaningful for people with diabetic neuropathy. We don’t know if those changes last a while or if they actually change people’s lives. Yet.

There haven’t been many studies in this area. So we don’t know more details than this yet, but it is promising. I treat many people with diabetes who are here for other things and will definitely be adding more balance and gait training to their programs to (hopefully) get some of these benefits.

What about specific hand and foot exercises?

The Gist -I’ll say up from that these turned out more questionable. Not useless, but not as clearly useful as the previous piece. And these have a little “OT energy” because of what they went for in their exercises and outcome measures. But overall, it’s good info and I like that it’s not unendingly positive like most papers are. It’s also our first ever paper from Myanmar, which is pretty cool.

This was an RCT where the participants did simple hand and foot exercises for 8 weeks while the control group received usual care and education about foot care. They used the Patient Neurotoxicity Questionnaire to check difficulty with ADLs like using utensils and phones, opening doors, climbing stairs, writing, walking, climbing stairs, tying shoes, and more. They also assessed pain with the VAS, hand strength with a grip dynamometer, and physical function with the TUG. This was done at 0, 8, and 16 weeks.

The exercises were all home-based 10 minute exercise sessions for both hands and feet. Hands were wrist and finger stretching, tendon glides, finger rolls, finger stretching, finger tapping, and a couple more. Foot exercises were foot tapping, ankle rotations, V-shape making with foot, and tennis ball rolling. All very simple stuff. They recommended people do the exercises 3 times per day for 8 weeks and kept track in exercise logbooks.

Outcome-wise, the intervention group significantly improved on their ability to perform work or chores, but nowhere else, even getting worse at their ability to use a spoon. The intervention group did improve at walking and climbing stairs, and that was the largest difference. Both groups improved in pain and they weren’t significantly different. And there were no significant differences on the TUG, with hand grip strength, or with pinch strength.

Tell Me More -I think this study shows (like quite a few others) if you don’t push people hard enough, they don’t improve. These were easy exercises that took very little time and they made very little difference. The difference they made on work and chores wasn’t nothing, but not huge either. The stair climbing and walking differences could make a real difference in these people’s lives and it’s nice they can be gained with relatively little effort since getting people on board to exercise hard can be hard in itself.

Overall, light exercises did make a little difference and heavier exercise would very likely make more of a difference in peripheral neuropathy. More evidence that we should do more of this.

What about the overall efficacy of PT on peripheral neuropathy?

The Gist -Pretty good, is what I can say up front. This systematic review broke down possible PT interventions into exercise therapy, electrotherapy, and “other treatments” and I’m going to focus on the exercise therapy since that’s more applicable to what we actually do.

It’s pretty well established that exercise like Tai Chi is effective for glucose control, lipid metabolism, and micro-circulation in the lower extremities. This review looked at progressive resistive exercise, active and passive stretching exercise, weight-bearing activities, and aerobic exercise to see how helpful they were in helped with neuropathy.

The studies that they pulled together found many benefits, let’s lay them out briefly:
- Strength and flexibility exercises of the ankle reduces peripheral neuropathic pain
- Whole body strength exercises improve foot structure and function
- All progressive resistive protocols reduced pain and disability
- General balance exercise was effective to improve gait and balance
- Active and passive ankle stretching reduced peak plantar pressures in the feet
- Weight bearing exercises like walking decreased disability, didn’t increase wounds, and increased overall fitness
- Aerobic exercise may decrease peripheral nerve degeneration
- Balance training for a few weeks could reduce neuropathic pain for months

Tell Me More -That’s a lot, I know. And not much of it is too surprising really, but it’s good to have on paper and see what we can do. The results above were each pulled from a study or two — there’s not a ton of specific research on this stuff — but it appears to be quite useful.

And that’s all I have to say on this. Bye!

What have we learned today? A summary.

Overall, these three studies show us that basically any type of PT can make a difference for people with diabetic peripheral neuropathy, but it needs to be challenging exercise. The most important part, to me, is that not only does exercise appear to improve the symptoms of neuropathy, like pain and disability, it could even slow the nervous breakdown itself. And not just via reducing blood sugar or HbA1c, but actually by stimulating the nerves and improving them. This is fantastic. Exercise has long been one of the major prescriptions for people with diabetes, but often for weight loss and sugar control, less for improving peripheral neuropathy. But it looks like it can and does.

If you want specific applications, that first paper is the best. It’s open access and has great appendices with exercise details, so dive into that to jump right into treatment.
