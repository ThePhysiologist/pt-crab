Let’s look inside the foot

The Gist -How often do you think about the intrinsic muscles of the foot? Not often, I’d guess. This piece from the journal Physical Therapy in Sport takes a deep dive into them. Did you know there are 22? Like, I learned that, but definitely forgot. I think we all remember that there are 4 layers though. Overall, it’s a well-written, easy to understand piece that goes into the anatomy, biomechanics, and function of the muscles while also talking a bit about how we strengthen them when needed.

Let’s start where we should start, with evaluation. There aren’t gold standard assessments of foot intrinsic strength, but there are some ways to go after it. You can check toe flexion strength with MMT, toe grip strength with a dynamometer, toe flexion ability with the paper test (which we’ll get to), and the “intrinsic positive test” checks out intrinsic strength overall. The last two may be new to you, so let’s look at them.

The paper grip test “is an easy to perform clinical test where the participant attempts to grip a piece of paper under the tested toe which the examiner attempts to pull away. This technique has not been validated to the authors knowledge and one limitation of this test is that it has been shown to activate both the intrinsics and extrinsic muscles”. Meanwhile, the intrinsic positive test, “includes having the participant extend the great toe, while simultaneously attempting to flex the lesser toes at the MTP joint and extend the interphalangeal joints. If the participant is able to perform this, it demonstrates strength of the intrinsics (lesser MTP flexion combined with interphalangeal joint extension). To the authors knowledge the validity and reliability of this qualitative test has not been published.”

Tell Me More -But what’s the point? We know “these muscles aren’t large contributors to force generation in gait,” even though they are active in walking. Rather, it’s speculated that intrinsics are important for foot shape and structure (which you probably already knew) and proprioception via stretch responses (which you may not have known). Weakness or atrophy of the intrinsics can lead to a lot of stuff including hammer toe, claw toe, pes cavus (which can echo up the kinetic chain), Charcot-Marie-Tooth, hallux valgus, pes planus, a loose hind foot in gait, plantar fasciitis, and more.

It’s been shown that diabetic neuropathy can lead to this atrophy, leading to a high incidence of hammer and claw toe deformities in this population.

Rehab-wise, there’s not too much honestly. You probably already know these. We’ve got short foot exercise in sitting and standing, the “toe piano” exercises where you extend the great toe while keeping the others down, then the 2nd-5th toes while keeping the great toe down, there’s resisted toe flexion with a theraband, toe spreading, and towel crunches. All of these have been shown to activate intrinsic muscles quite a bit and they can also improve arch height and limit navicular drop.

The paper has a lot more and it’s easy to read, like I said. So if you enjoyed this quick summary, I’d highly recommend it.

I’ve got nasty Hallux Valgus, what should I do?

The Gist - Probably surgery, if we’re honest. Or maybe at least. We don’t really know. This systematic review and meta-analysis tried to see how well non-surgical interventions worked for hallux valgus. They included 18 studies with “a wide range of non-surgical interventions for HV” and found that some may work but we don’t really know. “Five separate meta-analyses for foot orthoses, splints, manual therapy, and taping added to foot exercises showed no significant effects on primary outcomes,” those outcomes being foot pain and hallux valgus angle. 8 studies did show “a significant pain reduction with the use of foot orthoses, night splints, dynamic splints, manual therapy, taping added to foot exercises, multifaceted physical therapy program and Botox injections.” But as (almost) always the discrepancies stem from a lack of consistently good study design. The studies largely stink.

The abstract of the paper sums everything up pretty well: “There is a low level of certainty surrounding the effectiveness of non-surgical interventions for HV, however a reduction in pain appears more likely than improvement in HV angle.”

That doesn’t mean that we’re not going to talk about it more, cuz we will. Just don’t expect massive effects.

Tell Me More -I did learn a couple of quite interesting things from this paper. First that “HV I s prevalent and estimated to affect 23% of adults aged 18 to 65 years and 36% of adults aged over 65 years.” And that most interventions they looked at didn’t really work but the papers aren’t good enough to rule them out either. Pretty much the worst of both worlds. Two individual studies did demonstrate that toe spreading exercises could be helpful in decreasing pain and HV angle and that foot mobilization combined with toe spreading exercises could do those things as well, but that’s pretty much all we know.

Morton’s neuroma from, The Foot

The Gist -I’m pretty confident that this is the first article I’ve ever covered from The Foot, a journal dedicated to the topic that this Crab is dedicated to. This specific article is a case report on Morton’s neuroma because there isn’t too much out there about the conservative care thereof, at least according to the paper. The patient involved had unilateral dorsal foot pain for the last five years without any observed anatomical alterations even when checked out by 3 MRIs, 1 ultrasound, and 1 Xray. The official diagnosis was “highly-suggestive Morton’s Neuroma clinical presentation”.

In PT, she got 6 visits over the course of 9 weeks with a slowly advancing plan of STM, joint mobilizations, and exercises. Here’s the overall approach

Each session started with a 10-minute soft tissue massage for both the triceps surae muscles and the plantar fascia in order to improve vascularization and tone relaxation. Following that, several Grade IV mobilization techniques were performed as described in Chapter 16 of Maitland’s Peripheral Manipulation 4th Edition for the inferior tibiofibular, ankle and subtalar, tarsometatarsal and intermetatarsal joints. Then, active exercises in order to gain ankle dorsiflexion and strengthening of the foot’s intrinsic muscles using elastic resistance bands were instructed.

Over the course of treatment, the patient improved their dorsiflexion by 2.5cm in the knee to wall test, became negative in the Mulder’s click test, gained 20 points on the LEFS and 10 points on the FAAM, and decrease 22 points on the pain catastrophizing scale.

Tell Me More -I’m as annoyed as you are that the exercises aren’t well defined, but I think we can all guess at them especially in context of the first article of this edition. The discussion section of the piece refers to a few other studies that had a similar procedure and were effective using a combination of manual and ther ex, but this was the first that also looked into catastrophization beliefs too.

Obviously, this is a small case study and we need a lot more info before we know what’s what in this area. But, combined with the other papers cited in this one, it indicates that there’s a clear path forward for PT for Morton’s neuroma and treatment doesn’t all have to be surgical.

And since it’s just a case report, that’s kind of it for this week. Short, sweet, interesting. Bye!
