Psychology of ACL Rehab, PFJ Loading, Scapular Mobes, and Metabolic Syndrome

What are patients really saying? Listen between the lines.

The Gist -Researchers in the Journal of Athletic training wanted to learn more about how to support patients in the long process of ACL repair using qualitative interviews combined with patient reported outcomes to find out what patients were thinking as they recovered. In their words, “Capturing these…components is important because negative psychological responses may act as barriers to successful rehabilitative outcomes after ACL.” Here’s what they found:

Based on outcome scales, subjects were split into high and low perceived disability groups. Those in the high group had more internal and external barriers, like fear of reinjury and feelings of incapacity (internal) and limitations in coverage of treatment or treatment availability (external). Low disability groups tended to have more family and/or team support and an internal locus of control that high disability groups lacked.

Tell Me More -There’s a lot more in this open access paper so I highly recommend a full read. They use patient quotes to emphasize points and they’re quite powerful.

I think more mentally than physically, like I’m still cautious. The fear of retearing my ACL or doing something to screw up my surgery—I haven’t even gotten on a mountain bike since before my surgery because, is that one ride really worth being on crutches?
All patients, regardless of disability status, expressed negative emotional factors about some point of the process. Most patients expressed mental blocks to activity participation and fear of reinjury, but those in the low disability group were more effective at overcoming these feelings, though their methods of doing so remain opaque.

The biggest differences between the high and low disability groups were the facilitators to rehab. These were an internal locus of control, a stable support network, clear expectations of the recovery process, and fewer limitations during immediate postop rehab.

PFJ Pain? Lunge it back now ya’ll.

The Gist -This one sums up real quick. Want to reduce PFJ loading in a lunge? Make it a back lunge. All PFJ loading variables are lower in back lunge. Boo yah.

This comes up because “A forward lunge has been shown to be part of an effective exercise program for the treatment of PFP,” but can also cause PFJ stress, so the researchers wanted to find out if a back lunge would take that away, and it did. On the up motion of the lunge, back lunges produced 9.5% less stress than forward and the down portion produced 7.8% less. All research done in healthy women.

Tell Me More -As I said, this was done in healthy women who had no history of PFJ problems, so keep that in mind when generalizing. This hasn’t been tested at all in people with PFJ pain, but it is quite a positive result for them. The best, quick summary of all the data comes in the discussion section (usually the goldmine in papers), where they sum things up thus:

Findings support our hypothesis that the FL produces greater PFJ reaction force, greater quadriceps force PFJ loading rate, knee flexion, ankle dorsiflexion and trunk forward tilt compared to the BL. No difference was shown in PFJ stress.

Some reasons behind that are iterated farther down, where researchers report that the front lunge increased PFJ force due to an increase in quads force, knee flexion angle, and PFJ reaction force, with quads force reaching a maximum of 5.17 x body weight in this position.

Paper me.You got it. Not open access, but available here.

Unround that shoulder in more ways than one.

The Gist -We all know that pec minor stretching can be a good intervention for rounded shoulder posture, but what about scapular mobilizations? We weren’t sure until this paper in the Journal of Bodywork and Movement Therapies popped up last year. They popped participants into four treatment groups: pec minor stretching only, scapular mobes plus pec minor stretching, scapular mobes only, and control. After 5 sessions, they assessed the results.

Each group had about 10 healthy students with rounded shoulder postures at the beginning, and all but the control got better about equally at the end. Posture was assessed via motion capture and showed that all three intervention groups decreased scapular internal and downward rotation about the same amount while the control did not. Grade IV scapular retraction, upward rotation, and distraction made those improvements.

Tell Me More -I don’t think I mentioned above, but this was an RCT, so well done there. The therapists were blinded to outcome and the patients to allocation. While differences in all intervention groups were about the same, the combined mobe and stretch group decreased scapular anterior tilt more than the others. All of them made significant differences in scapular resting position. The researchers speculate that the lack of difference is due to all interventions being based on the same mechanism and the relatively low number of intervention periods. In that regard, it’s a bit of a pilot study. It’s also a bit limited due to the lack of review of scapular kinematics (resting position only) and the use of all healthy patients.

A Heavier Adolescents Less Healthy?

The Gist -Hello pedes and school therapists, here’s one for you. Researchers in the Journal of Pediatric Physical Therapy wanted to find out if overweight or obese adolescents had less healthy metabolic systems when controlling for physical fitness. So they worked it out. After putting their groups of adolescents through a battery of physical tests including BP, fasting glucose, HDL and triglyceride levels, VO2max testing, DEXA scans, and more, they determined that indeed, overweight and obese adolescents are less metabolically healthy than health weight adolescents even when controlling for physical fitness.

Tell Me More -46 adolescents ended up in the study, with 30 meeting normal weight criteria and 16 meeting overweight/obese criteria based on BMI. To check metabolic health, they compared glucose levels, insulin levels, insulin resistance, HDL levels, triglycerides, C-reactive protein, and the overall, Metabolic Syndrome Severity Score. Is was this MSSS that was the real determining factor, trending almost directly with BMI, with a Person correlation coefficient of 0.816.

Insulin resistance, SBP, and insulin levels were the significant differences between the normal and overweight groups, with insulin resistance being the real concern for metabolic syndrome. The last bit of interesting information is that the researchers found that waist circumference >80th percentile for sex and age was an extremely efficient predictor of metabolic health. This is great, since it’s an easy measure to take in a clinic and gives you a lot of data straight away.

Have a read of this. Cuz it’s quite a good paper and open access for you underpaid pediatric PTs (so, all of you).
