Issue 106

Rotator Cuff Interesting Stuff

Did you know I’ve never really covered the rotator cuff in the Crab? I mean, you would know if you’re an outpatient PT who has been sitting there waiting for this content for over two years (did you know I’ve been doing this for over two years? Time flies when you’re just living your normal life as you coast toward deaths’ door.). Since it’s important and we all have one (some of us even have two gasp), it’s time to talk about them.

This week we’re digging into early vs. late mobilization of the cuff, the results of a 12 week exercise therapy RCT on surgical repairs, and PT access disparities between people with Medicaid and those with private insurance who have rotator cuff pathology. There are also some brief notes on four more studies (including one about table tennis) because I feel bad about not covering this before.

Let’s dive in!

Early Active Movement Post-Surgery Is Just As Good As Not

The Gist -With that loooonnngg title out of the way, the details on this are really about how you spin it. This was a systematic review and meta-analysis of 8 RCTs with 756 participants that compared early active shoulder movement (\<6 weeks) to late movement (>6 weeks active ROM restricted, used sling). In the briefest of briefness, there weren’t any significant differences between the groups in pain in motion or at rest, Western Ontario Rotator Cuff Index score, Constant score, shoulder ROM, strength, and cuff integrity (including the amount of retears) at any time point beyond 3 months. A few studies ran measurements of these factors out to two years and reported no significant between-group differences. Really. Things get fuzzier at less than three months, and I’ll get into that in the section below.

For now, it’s most important to point out that the clinical benefits of early movement are uncertain. There isn’t a difference, so maybe we should do it? Or maybe there isn’t a difference, so we should keep doing what we’re doing? Obviously, many of you are bound by physician protocol anyway but it’s all very interesting information. The most important piece overall comes in the conclusion to the paper: “There were no adverse effects associated with early active shoulder movement.” That’s great.

Tell Me More -This is where we look closer than 3 months and see what the differences were in the first 6 weeks or so.

There was no difference in pain at rest or with activity in the first 6 weeks or three months, and no difference in pain at night in the first 6 weeks either (this was the only time period in which night pain was measured).

The WORC index showed moderate certainty evidence that early active movement lowered it at 6 weeks with those differences tapering away by 3 months.

ROM showed 16° greater active flexion at 6 weeks in the early active movement group but those differences too tapered away and disappeared at 3 months. Abduction was also greater at 6 weeks, as was ER, and again those disappeared afterward. There wasn’t a significant IR difference at 6 weeks.

Lastly, integrity. It was measured via ultrasound in 2 studies, MRI in 2 studies, and was unspecified in the other that reported it. They checked retear rates with these imaging modalities and saw that there wasn’t a difference at any time point.

I see your early active and raise you an early activity protocol.

The Gist -This study looked into the difference between a 12 week early active protocol and usual care after rotator cuff surgery in the aptly titled (but not aptly capitalized) CUT-N-MOVE trial. 2 weeks post-surgery for a traumatic full-thickness rotator cuff tear, half of the participants got to work with AAROM and AROM while others waited 6 weeks to get started. Beyond that in weeks 2-5, the experimental group had 3 PT sessions per week along with home exercises while the control group saw a PT once a week with an HEP as well. From weeks 6-12 both groups had PT 2x/wk along with HEPs and they were progressed through exercises throughout according to the judgment of a PT with the goal being continuous increase in motion and load throughout. There’s a specific protocol in their pilot study.

At 12 weeks, there wasn’t a significant difference between the groups on their primary outcome (the WORC score) nor on any of their secondary outcomes (pain, ROM, re-tear, strength, DASH, and Global Rating Scale). Of the 82 participants, 9 had retears in the first 12 weeks, 6 in the aggressive group and 3 in the control. At 1 year, a total of 6 in the aggressive group and 7 in the control had retears of their cuff. Overall, there wasn’t a difference.

So why am I pulling this one out individually? Because they had an aggressive training protocol that the researchers thought would really make a difference. And it didn’t. Neither positively nor negatively.

Tell Me More -Their retear rate (16%) may seem high, but it’s apparently on par with many other studies. Both groups improved satisfactorily based on previously published criteria and patients were largely happy with their progress according to the Global Rating Scale.

Overall, it looks like the data is in and aggressive early motion is out. But again, it’s based on how you spin it. What’s nice about all of this is that we can take away some of the fear that patients have with early motion. Protocols that say “no motion before 6 weeks” can frighten people and the knowledge that we can actually do active ROM at just two weeks or less and experience no more adverse events or hold back rehab at all is great news. But early movement just isn’t the shortcut to improvement we had hoped it to be.

Want an appointment? Have Medicaid? Go somewhere else.

The Gist - This is going to be a briefer take because it’s a briefer article. It’s just here to point out that PT services for people on Medicaid are not up to snuff. They called 465 PT practices, at which 52.7% accepted Medicaid for a theoretical person getting rehab after rotator cuff surgery. 95% were ready to take a patient with private insurance. The median wait to an appointment was 2 days, but Medicaid varied from 0-72(!) days while private insurance from 0-43. They called practices all across the US and saw that people in both Medicaid expansion states and those who haven’t expanded since the ACA were pretty similar in their acceptance and wait times. PT practices that did accept Medicaid were in ZIP codes with lower median incomes (57K vs. 61K). Most practices didn’t elaborate on why they wouldn’t take a patient beyond having “no contract” with Medicaid.

I’m not going to rail on here about how we need to do better for people on Medicaid, because it’s obvious that we do. What I’m going to do instead is point you to Ryan Shelton, the owner of PhilanthroPT, an outpatient clinic in Kentucky that doesn’t turn away anyone on Medicaid and is still financially successful. He’s very active on Twitter talking about how we all need to do better to help these people. Because we do.
