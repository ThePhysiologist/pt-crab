Issue 183

Cognition

This isn’t physical therapy. Directly, at least. But it still matters a lot. We’re talking dementia again to double down on it. This time, it’s not what we can do with it, but how we can diagnose it. And yes, it’s not our job to diagnose people with dementia. I know. But we get a lot of access to our clients, much more than most healthcare providers, so we have a good chance to spot early signs and direct people to further testing. It’d be good to know what to look for.

With that, let’s dive in!

How many people are cognitively impaired in your clinic?

The Gist -Quite a few. And more than in the general population. This was a paper about Medicare beneficiaries who received outpatient PT in 2016. They looked through the charts 730 Medicare beneficiaries in 2016 as part of the Health and Retirement Study. The goal was to figure out how many of these folks had normal cognitive status, cognitive impairment without dementia, or just plain old dementia.

The HRS Cognitive Scale was used to separate people into these three categories “via tests of immediate and delayed recall of 10 common nouns, serial subtractions by 7, and a backward count task from 20.” There was a scoring algorithm used, but we’re not going to get into that right now. Results-wise, they found that 20.3% of people presenting to PT had cognitive impairment. 75% of these had cognitive impairment and 25% had dementia. In the general population, about 11% of Medicare beneficiaries have cognitive impairment, so those who seek PT have it at about double the rate of normal.

To put a very fine point on it, 20% of people on Medicare who come to your clinic have cognitive impairment. The rate was even higher in people who were Black (37%), over 85 years old (50%), and had less than a high school education (63%). It was also a bit higher for those who performed moderate to vigorous exercise less than twice a week, at 27% in this population.

Tell Me More -What does this mean for you? It means the next section of the newsletter is even more important than one would think. It also means that you may want to simplify your instructions and information to improve adherence and effectiveness.

It also means that you may want to up your game when it comes to dementia and decline screening. The next section has details on that, I highly recommend it. You can save literal years of decline by catching this early, so start thinking about it actively.

Let’s do some testing

The Gist -Let’s practice at the top of our licenses real quick. We’re increasingly primary care providers, and that’s great. It also brings extra challenges. As a DPT, you’re aware of some of these already. Red flags, screening for cancer, you know the stuff. We must know how to refer out. I see the area of dementia care as an opportunity, not a challenge. We spend a lot of time with our patients, more than most people in healthcare. So we have a great chance to see things that others don’t. And if we spot them, here’s how we can test them.

The Alzheimer’s Association put together an excellent package that’s designed to be used at the annual wellness visit for Medicare, but it can be used just about anywhere. It includes a decision algorithm and two quick screening tools that take just 5 minutes to administer. You probably learned some of these in school (like I did) but don’t know which to administer and when (like me), and this will set you right. Let’s get into it.

First off, the flowchart. It’s pretty simple. If there are signs and symptoms present, administer both a patient assessment and informant assessment (if possible). If there aren’t signs and symptoms present, confirm with an informant. If you can’t confirm, administer the tests.

Tell Me More -The recommended tests are the GPCOG and the Mini-COG. Both take less than 5 minutes to administer and are relatively free from cultural and educational bias. I’ll link to these resources below. They both involved some memory tasks, clock drawing, and recall and have easy cutoffs to determine if the patient needs further assessment.

Also in the package are two screenings for the informant to take, asking them about how their relative or friend has changed in the last 5-10 years. One of these is the AD8, an 8 question scale with a sensitivity of 84% and specificity of 80% used on its cutoffs. Not perfect, but a pretty good, quick test.

What do you do after that? There are a few possible steps. That depend on the knowledge and relationships you have.

You can refer back to a primary care physician. You can refer to a neurologist, gerontologist, or neuropsychologist if you know some in your area. You can also direct to resources like the Alzheimer’s helpline, a 24/7 line to guide people with the disease. That’s at 800-272-3900. Or you can direct to a website with resources, the community resource finder at Alzheimers.org. alz.org/CRF

So let’s get you those tools, huh? You can see the whole cognitive assessment toolkit here, it’s the best place to start. Alz.org has a significant amount of information as well and I highly recommend it to everyone.

And that’s our week! Thanks for tuning in and I hope this changes something about how you practice. Catching dementia early is massive, please try. It’s so important.

Bye!
