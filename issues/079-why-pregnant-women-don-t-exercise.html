Why pregnant women don’t exercise

The Gist -This open access article from the Journal of Physiotherapy is a systematic review of 47(!) studies with 7,655 participants. There was news, good and bad. Good, pregnant women have positive attitudes toward physical activity and they want to do it. Bad, the barriers are what you would expect and hard to deal with. They’re mainly lack of time and some pregnancy discomfort as well. We obviously can’t fix lack of time, but could help with pregnancy discomfort. Beyond that, there’s more good.

One of the main enablers to exercise while pregnant is information about adult and fetal health benefits. That’s definitely an area for PT to help. The researchers say that “A person-centred approach may facilitate translation of the positive attitudes of pregnant women into increased physical activity participation during pregnancy and therefore may be more effective than education alone.” Other enablers? Mainly interpersonal support and pregnancy-specific programs, also areas where PT can help.

Tell Me More -Since there were 47 studies to work with, the authors were also able to dig into socio-cultural themes that emerged. Specifically, “for women from low-income areas, addressing affordability and access to a safe place for physical activity are key to enabling these women to participate in physical activity.” Additionally, “Women in studies focusing on specific ethnic and cultural groups expressed concerns about safety of physical activity”. The researchers also noted that women who are overweight and obese saw pregnancy-specific exercise groups as more enabling than women of healthy weights.

The researchers recommend that exercise professionals get involved in antenatal visits on an individual level and also at the group level. Something like this could even be turned into an independent business if a PT was so inclined.

The whole paper is a wealth spring of information in a readable format rarely offered by systematic reviews, so give it a read if you have any interest.

How far do your nerves actually glide?

The Gist -This cadaveric study digs into the basic science that few PT Crabs look into, but I found it quite interesting and think you will too. This research group obtained 10 male cadavers who didn’t have upper extremity problems or previous surgery when they died, exposed their radial, ulnar, and median nerves, added some fancy tracking tools, then did nerve glides with them to see just how far the nerves glid. It’s quite a simple study and also quite cool. After going through the full glides, they found that the median nerves glided an average of 11.37mm, ulnar made it 13.5mm, and radials 29.75mm. In inch terms, that’s about 3/8”, 5/8”, and 1 1/4”, respectively. How cool!

Tell Me More -Here’s a less cool downside, the researchers don’t tell us how long the arms of the cadavers are, so we don’t know how far the nerves glide relative to the arm length. Still, we do know that they glide and can glide quite significantly. The range on the median nerve was 9-21mm and ulnar was 7-29.

Previous research has used ultrasound to show that the sciatic nerve moves 10mm during an SLR and the median can move 8mm when doing carpal tunnel exercises, so this isn’t completely new research, but it gives us a different perspective since we can directly see the nerves in a cadaveric study. Overall it’s simple and interesting.

Needles for Patellar Tendinopathy? If you want…

The Gist -This relatively small RCT (n=48) looked into three groups of people with patellar tendinopathy. These groups were dry needling with eccentric exercise, percutaneous needle electrolysis with eccentric exercise, and eccentric exercise with sham needling to see which was best. The 42 men and 6 women, average age 32 were checked for improvement in disability and pain at weeks 10 and 22 after going through daily eccentric exercises and four needling sessions in 8 weeks.

At 10 and 22 weeks, all groups improved significantly, but there was no significant between-group difference on disability. By week 22, 54% of participants had improved enough to say that they had reached “good function” (>80 points on the VISA-p). On the pain front, all groups improved significantly, with the electric needle group improving just a touch more than the other two (1.7 vs. 1.3 for DN only and 1.0 for the eccentric only).

Tell Me More -As part of this study, they also took imaging ultrasound of the tendon structure at baseline and again at weeks 10 and 22. At baseline, the mean thickness was 0.65cm. This shrunk to 0.61cm by the end of the study. Neovascularization also reduced throughout the study, from 44% of participants to just 31% at the end. Between groups though, there were no significant differences on tendon thickness or vascularization.

On the downside for this whole study, their blinding didn’t work very well. Everyone in the electric group successfully guessed which group they were in, potentially confounding the whole thing. We also have the problem of 42 men and 6 women and the small size of each arm overall. Still, this is a first of its kind so hopefully people grow and improve it over time.

How Patients Feel About Getting Out of Bed

The Gist -This open access article (hint, go read it cuz it’s qualitative) discusses how patients experience their first mobilizations after abdominal surgery. To cut things really short, the overarching theme was “I’ll do whatever it takes to get home earlier.” Which is great! They sampled what 23 patients had to say about being mobilized immediately after abdominal surgery at a hospital in Sweden, taking interviews 1 to 4 days after surgery.

In general, patients had a positive response to mobilization, even those who were nervous about it in the beginning. They found that it helped clear their brains and that pain and tenderness after mobilization were less than expected. In general, “Mobilization did not increase pain; on the contrary, physical motion was experienced as reducing and gaining control of pain.” On the function and mental side then, it went well and patients were mostly down with it. That’s great news that’s backed up by many patient quotes in the paper.

Tell Me More -Even though patients were down with it, they were nervous as well. Here’s a great quote about that nervousness and how it was cleared up:

Yeah, first I thought dear me, do they really know what they’re doing? But then as I said (she) had a very confident way and she also said that, you’re in good hands, I can look at you and see whether you can do it or not. That’s about how she explained it. So, if I can or if I can’t, we’ll just stop. That’s about how she explained it, so I had complete faith in her.

At this hospital, patients were told about early mobilization pre-op, lowering the barrier to it afterward. “Participants never protested against early mobilization” according to the paper. I’m sure this isn’t the case in your hospital but it’s great news for this one. I’ll leave you with one last, big quote and then be off.

Patients are in need of individualized information about the process they are going through to be able to take control of their situation. In addition to that, participants in the present study pointed out the importance of getting information in advance about the procedure and especially about how their body could react to mobilization.
