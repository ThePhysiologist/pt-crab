Does PNF Help Balance? We think so.

The Gist -This small, speculative study is following up on a case report from a couple of years ago when PTs used PNF exercises to improve the balance of a stroke survivor. These PTs wanted to start building a bundle of research on PNF, so they started in the easiest way possible, testing the balance of 30 healthy volunteers, putting 15 through 6 weeks of upper and lower extremity PNF exercise training and the other through nothing. After this, they tested their balance again and boom the exercised were a bit better. Look, this isn’t ground-breaking research, but it is the first of its kind and shows that PNF exercise could be helpful. Only time will tell overall, but at least we know it doesn’t hurt and probably does help.

Tell Me More -The study is clearly limited through its small, young, and healthy sample since this is a starting point for these exercises. In lieu of going any deeper here, I’m going to talk a bit about the case report from 2016. A 69 year old male with right hemiplegia for 17 years had diminished balance and balance confidence. They put him through 3h of PNF therapy per week for 6 weeks, in which time they improved his 10MWT to 0.82m/s from 0.67, and cut three seconds of his five time sit to stand. That was our groundbreaker in this field, so watch this space for more studies on PNF for balance, they’re about to start flooding in.

If You Make ‘Em Think, They’ll Exercise More

The Gist -From the Archive of PM&R, this interesting bit of research tested 14 people with a history of discectomy by putting them under cognitive load while exercising. Researchers were trying to see if they could improve exercise performance and reduce fear of falling by giving participants a cognitive task. In this case, counting backward from 300 by 7s (I tried, it’s hard). Under the dual-task condition, with exercise and cognitive load combined, participants reported similar pain, but increased their exercise ability by 57% in the prone bridging task and 23% on the Biering-Sorenson test. Nice!

Tell Me More -Researchers speculate that participants were able to increase exercise time due to them having a “dissociative focus” to distract them from the main task being performed. Since time to failure on the Biering-Sorenson is associated with kinesiophobia, they hope these participants could have reduced kinesiophobia when dual-tasking, but we don’t know that for sure yet. Since increasing exercise time is generally seen as a better approach than increasing intensity, this is really beneficial for getting more from your patients. Consider trying this out in other populations as well.

What do people with Parkinson’s want to do? Exercise.

The Gist -This big discrete choice study from Australia gave us some interesting data in what people with Parkinson’s are looking for as they live with the disease. They gave 540 people a survey to see what choices they’d make if they could pick any exercise prescription. Some results were obvious and some much less so. Here’s what they got:
- People working less than 300 minutes per week were willing to add more time to their weekly exercise
- Men were happy to add more resistance exercise
- Women didn’t want to add aerobic exercise
- Both men and women wanted more multimodal exercise
- Fitness instructors with PD experience were preferred to PTs with PD experience as exercise leaders.

Tell Me More -This study may not be completely applicable to the U.S. as it was done in a well-educated, Australian population, but it does offer some clues as to ways to help patients with PD. The multimodal exercise and amount of exercise preferences are definitely useful, especially for patients who understand the interplay between exercise and PD treatment. This was also a group of people with moderate to low severity PD, so different results may come with more advanced disease states. Overall, give them more and give them multiple. If some is good, more must be better.

How What You Think Affects What They Do.

The Gist -Researchers looked into SNFs to see how patient and provider beliefs affected probability of successful community discharge. They do! In interesting ways. Positive patient beliefs led to a 6% higher probability of successful discharge regardless of disease state. This was true regardless of staff’s belief in the patients, but they could make things a bit better or worse. If both staff and the patient felt positively about their likelihood of successful discharge, probability increased by 2.5%, but if staff felt positively and patients felt negatively, the probability of discharge was reduced by 4.6%. Interesting indeed. So, you can make a difference, but you need to understand where the patient is and try to bring them along with you to make the biggest difference.

Tell Me More -This study was HUUUUGGGGEEEE! It covered 526,432 Medicare beneficiaries aged 66 or over in SNFs for stroke, hip fracture, or TBI. They picked those conditions because “their functional limitations with self-care and mobility are the primary reasons for post acute care referral.” I’m not really sure how that justifies the choice, but I think they were going with patients who had a fairly clear path to recovery. Oh! Above, I didn’t specifically say what the beliefs were about. Lucky you for reading this section, huh? Nice job. Specifically, the beliefs were about a patient’s capacity to increase independence in ADLs. Lastly, the outcome measure was “successful discharge from the SNF within 90 days of admission.” Successful discharge was counted as going home without readmission to any inpatient facility or death for at least 30 days. Now you know!
