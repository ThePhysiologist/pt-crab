Squat! Squat! Squat! Squat! Squat-Squat!

*in the rhythm of the Shot song, whatever it’s called

One more deep dive into squats this week to complete the process. Last week, we talked about biomechanics of squats. This one, we’re talking about how to tweak those biomechanics and why you might want to. It’s squats for all at the PT Crab bar this week.

We’ll open by talking about a classic, what the resistance band around the knees actually does for a squat. Anything? Nothing? Bad things? Wait and see. Then we’re looking at what effect abdominal activation has in changing squat dynamics. It does make quite a difference to some parts and all but none to others. And lastly, what’s wrong with stooping, really? Last week we talked about the force dynamics of the stoop lift, this week we’re going to figure out if anyone should be stooping. Or maybe if everyone should be.

Dive in for details.

What does a TheraBand around the knees do?

The Gist -We all learned it, we all use it. But does it do what we think it does? The goal (usually) is to prevent medial knee collapse with a squat. That and/or increase the hip abduction work required in a squat. Unfortunately, there are conflicting findings about how useful this actually is. This narrative review wanted to figure out the answer.

First off, why do we care? Ever wondered why we care about controlling dynamic knee valgus? Valgus creates lateral compression, medial distraction, which “may increase the risk of developing lower extremity injuries, including anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) ruptures, patellofemoral pain, knee osteoarthritis, and knee cartilage and meniscus damage.” BUT, “Conversely, there is some evidence illustrating that medial knee collapse may not be a risk factor for knee injuries. Regarding ACL injuries specifically, Cronström and colleagues performed a meta-analysis of longitudinal studies that had performed various baseline knee kinematic and/or kinetic assessments during weight-bearing activities; injury metrics were collected from participants at a later date. Contrary to current clinical opinions, no kinematic or kinetic measure accurately predicted future ACL injury.” So we don’t know. But we think it’s better not to have this collapse. So let’s not!

The point of the band is explained via Reactive Neuromuscular Training. The band provides a force that must be reacted to, increasing external rotation force to be applied while squatting. The theory is that this force will be “remembered” when the band is removed, thus preventing future collapse. The 9 studies this review looked into analyzed the effect of the bands on barbell back squats, bodyweight squats, overhead barbell squats, countermovement jumps, squat jumps, and drop jumps to see what effect they had.

Results were mixed, as usually. But generally speaking glute med and max forces were increased with the addition of the band. Some saw kinematic changes, some did not. Surprisingly, the knee valgus kinematic change spotted most often was an increase in knee valgus, rather than a decrease.

In short, it appears that the band generally increases glute max and med activity and may decrease some quads activity in the squat.

Tell Me More -Isn’t it bad if it causes knee valgus to increase? I’m glad I made you ask. No. Probably. Since these folks won’t always be doing squats with the TheraBand, increasing the knee valgus angle just while it’s on them isn’t necessarily a a bad thing. It means the bands are a tool for strength gains in the hip external rotators and extensors rather than a tool toward creating proper form. Which is fine. Just know what tools to use and when.

The fact that the bands can cause medial collapse also points out that you need to dose their use properly. Use them in order to strengthen external rotators and abductors, not to correct form. Use bands that are light enough so as not to cause a large increase in knee valgus, especially with dynamic activities like countermovement and drop jumps. One of the studies used yellow through black Theraband during countermovement jumps and found that the black band increased knee valgus and did not increase muscle activity. It was just too strong to overcome, so the muscles didn’t. And lastly, remember that the point of applying an external force like this is not to help someone perform the exercise correctly while the band is being applied, it’s to help them get it right afterward and strengthen those muscles.

Ab Squeeze before a squat increases core work and decreases lumbar forces

The Gist -Like the paper above, this one wanted to see if adding something to a squat could make it better. They taught participants to use the”abdominal bracing maneuver” where to make a muscle contraction around the entire trunk before beginning a motion.

26 healthy men were recruited to perform squats with and without this maneuver and see what the difference was. The cue for the maneuver was to “Gently inhale, then exhale, now stiffen your trunk as though you will be hit in the belly. Hold this contraction.” Squats were tracked with 3D marker analysis, a force plate, and EMG throughout the trunk and legs. Participants performed three squats under four conditions: front squat with bracing, front squat without bracing, back squat with bracing, and back squat without bracing. Back squats were done at 75% of predicted 1 rep max and front squats were done at 75% of that weight.

What they found in brief:
- Trunk muscle activity did not generally increase during the squat when the abdominal bracing was applied
- Lower extremity activation did not generally change when the condition was applied
- Spinal position did change, with lumbar extension increasing when bracing was applied.
- Squats with abdominal bracing were performed more slowly
- Trunk muscle activity was very different between front and back squats, with front squats having much higher trunk muscle activity and less lumbar extension than back.
- Back squats had higher forces at the hip and ankle joints than front, while front squats had higher forces at the knee

Tell Me More -It’s surprising that trunk activity didn’t change, but nice that posture did. The motion analysis showed decreased lumbar loads when abdominal bracing was applied due to the increased lumbar extension. This implies that the bracing maneuver could be better for the lumbar spine, since it holds it in a more neutral position and decreases shear loads in the area.

Why didn’t muscle activity change? They were using pretty high weights (which is great!) so trunk bracing was happening regardless of preparatory activity. Lower extremity muscle activity didn’t change overall between back and front squats, even at a lower weight, it just shifted around from hips and ankles to knees. Front squats at 75% of back squat weight provide the same training impetus as back squats at the full weight. This is nice because one can get a larger impetus with a smaller load. Always a good thing when lifting.

I often front squat because I don’t like being under and extremely heavy bar and because of how the bar bruises my back. Front squats are tough on my wrists, but it’s nice to know that I can probably lower the weight a bit more than I already do.

Lastly, speed. The bracing slowed down the squats, which could be good. A past study found that increased squat speed causes higher tibiofemoral A-P shear forces and compressive forces. If one “bounces” at the bottom of the squat (as you will often see with fast squatting), the compressive force can go up by as much as 33% more. Faster lifting speed also increases the lumbar spine flexion moment and lumbar shear.

The authors think that speed was slowed due to increased task vigilance. That is, the squatters were paying more attention to what they were doing, so they went more slowly. This means that once the behavior becomes completely learned, squats may speed back up.

Overall, if you want to load the trunk more, do a front squat. If you want to slow a squat down and decrease spinal flexion, add an abdominal contraction. If you want to load the hips more, use a back squat. If you want to leave work early to sip piña coladas because it’s almost summer and you deserve it, do that.

Lift with your back. It’s fine. *

*If you don’t have a back problem
The Gist -”Lift with your legs!” They shout. Any time they get a chance. But if it’s so much better to lift with your legs, why do we naturally lift with our backs? The so-called stoop lift. Shouldn’t we know better? These clinicians argue that we do know better. We know that lifting with your back isn’t bad. And that we should be training people how to do it safely because they’re going to do it.

Ready for a fun fact? “The squat technique is the most commonly advised lifting technique by health care providers, however, it has been shown that training on squat lifting does not prevent LBP and utilizing greater lumbar flexion during lifting (i.e. stoop lifting) is not a risk factor for LBP onset, persistence, or recurrence.” So there.

What these writers argue is that we should be choosing the lifting technique that “optimizes movement to complete the task at hand.” Movement is multi-factorial, they argue, and should be considered in four contexts, “1) metabolic, 2) biomechanical, 3) physical stress tolerance, and 4) pain.

Metabolic - Stoop lifting (AKA lift with your back) is the lowest energy consumption method when compared to squat lifting and semi-squat lifting (refer to last week’s edition for a photo of these). If someone needs to preserve work capacity because they’re going to be doing something all day, the stoop lift may be the better choice.

Biomechanical - We learned about this last week already. From a treatment perspective, assign the lift that targets the muscles you want to target. Squat will target knee and hip extensors, stoop will get lumbar and hip extensors, and semi-squat will be a combo. As an example of one, I do a lot of work in the garden and with DIY projects in the yard. Building fences, decks, patios, shoveling, pulling weeds, the lot. I’m not going to squat down for everything. I’m going to stoop as often as possible. It’s easier. So I need to train stoop lifting if I want to stay healthy and do it well. And your patients may need to as well. It does increase shear loads on the lumbar spine, so it may not be the best choice if they already have pain there, but more on that in a second.

Physical stress tolerance - Increased stress causes tissue to develop. Too much hurts tissue. We know. There is some evidence that stoop lifting can increase disc herniations. But if stoop lifting is trained, if muscles, tendons, and capsules are strengthened properly over time, one can stoop lift without herniation risk, the authors argue. They say that “when considering modification of a patient’s lifting technique, clinicians must consider whether it was the technique itself or the inadequate dosage of physical stress that led to injury. it is the clinician’s job to find the optimal dosage of physical stress to ensure hypertrophy and minimize the risk of injury.” That doesn’t mean to avoid the stoop, it means one should train the stoop properly and build it up over time.

Pain - Pain should lead one to modify lifting techniques temporarily, they argue, not long term. Give the tissue a break when it hurts, then go back to other lifting techniques, training them up properly. “An alternate lifting technique as a temporary de- sensitizer can be used to calm tissue down prior to build- ing tissue up. Even in cases of chronic pain, alternate lifting techniques can be used to teach the patient that they can control their pain and positively affect overall movement tolerance.”

Tell Me More -This paper is great. It’s less educational and more a view into a thought process. I loved reading it and think you should too. There’s a lot more in there, but this is a long edition so I’m stopping here. Except for this other fun thing from the acknowledgements, “The authors would like to thank Samford University’s Doctor of Physical Therapy class of 2023 for engaging in a lively class debate which served as the genesis for this manuscript.” Out of the mouths of babes, it seems.
