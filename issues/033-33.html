What Functional Tests Should I Use for Concussion?

The Gist -Well, we don’t really know yet, but this paper has some ideas. Researchers grabbed athletes who sustained sports concussions out of the ER and tested them at one week post-concussion, at return to activity (as determined by their physicians), and two weeks post return to activity. They compared this to healthy athletes tested at similar time points. The average time away from the field with concussion is just 21 days, so this was a fairly short study.

Results? They ran everyone through the Y Balance Test and the Multiple Hop Test. Concussed folks didn’t differ on the Y Balance, but did on MHT, specifically with how many times they needed a second foot to stabilize their landings. This held through the return to activity determination, but they were equal to non-concussed at two weeks post-RTA. MHT could be a good functional test when assessing ability to return to sport.

Tell Me More -Real quick, the multiple hop test is about dynamic postural control. People hop and land in a multi-directional pattern, stabilizing between each hop. They’re not supposed to dab (neither the ground, nor the way Cam Newton did) between jumps.

There weren’t any significant differences on the Y Balance Test, but the MHT dabbing was the differentiator until two weeks post return to activity. The researchers didn’t control return to activity, they just went with the determination made by an independent doctor. Because of how MHT changed throughout the study, it could be a good test to assess dynamic control and determine when people are really ready to return to sport.

Paper please.Gotcha.

One Simple Trick to Fix Motion Sensitivity

The Gist -This single-blind, RCT showed that you can probably make inroads in motion sensitivity rehab with one, simple exercise. Their 40 participants in two groups did either gaze stability exercises (stare at a spot and move head back and forth — intervention group) or really slow saccades (control group). Those who did the intervention scored better on postural control (in a cool, VR test), the motion sickness susceptibility questionnaire, and an anxiety questionnaire. Nice!

Tell Me More -This paper suffers from a bit of outcome measure fatigue, as they piled them on in looking for a result. That doesn’t invalidate what they did get, but it’s just something to note. At the end of the day, the MSQ is a regularly used motion sickness questionnaire and improvement on that is great news, especially with such a simple exercise.

Limitation-wise, this was a small sample size, they’re not certain everyone stuck to the HEP (they had to do it every day and it got progressively harder), and this study was only done in people aged 20-40, so it may not be generalizable to an older population. This whole area probably needs more work, but it was a simple study to pull off, so hopefully more is coming our way.

Paper me please.Gotcha 2.

Low K is OK. High K is Too.

The Gist -This piece from the Journal of Acute Care PT assessed whether it was safe to mobilize patients with slightly high or slightly low potassium and good news! It is! Normal potassium is 3.5-5.0mmol/L, and they exercised people who were moderately hypokalemic (3.1-3.4) and moderately hyperkalemic (5.1-5.9) and reported no serious adverse events in 380 patient interactions. There were minor adverse events (dizziness, shortness of breath, etc.) in 2% of cases, but all of those were in the normal potassium group.

Tell Me More -I probably don’t have to tell you, but increased or decreased potassium can equal cardiac arrhythmia, not a great thing. Hence, PTs can be nervous about mobilizing patients who aren’t normal. The Henry Ford Hospital, where this study took place, allows mobilization of patients who are between 3.1 and 5.9mmol/L, so all these interactions were within safety margins according to the hospital, but it was important to track it and find out if these things were indeed safe. Clinicians were concerned due to the increased myocardial demand brought about by activity, especially when walking patients and whatnot. Fortunately, it looks like things may be okay.

Is Your Labral Tear Patient Really Ready for Sport? Probably Not…

The Gist -This sobering study looked into measures of shoulder strength after “anterior and/or posterior labral surgery for traumatic dislocation or subluxation injury” and compared them to normal return to sport guidelines and tests 6 months post injury. Most of the 43 patients failed at least one of the strength tests (90% of uninvolved side = passing grade) even when they passed functional tests. Only 12 of the 43 were able to match external rotation strength goals and 38 of 43 failed at least one of the tests. Since most guidelines for return to sport after this surgery at time-based (usually 5-6 months), all these failures are really bad news.

Tell Me More -All patients went through a standardized rehab protocol that’s in the paper, and all were tested over 6 months after their injuries. But if they weren’t strong, why did they pass functional tests? Specifically, they tested using the CKCUES test (a pushup position hand alternating touch test (just look it up :P)) and the Unilateral Seated Shot-put test. The researchers speculate that the athletes were able to compensate using other musculature, helping them pass the functional tests. The researchers finish with a reflection on how this is similar to ACL repair a few years ago and where we could go from here:

The Delaware-Oslo ACL cohort demonstrated disappointing results with standardized testing at 6 months, with 90% failing the return to sport testing at 6 months. Meanwhile, greater than 50% of the Delaware-Oslo ACL cohort reported normal knee function on subjective measures. Importantly, this study demonstrated that reinjury was substantially reduced with the delay in return to sport to 9 months irrespective of the results of the return to sport testing.
