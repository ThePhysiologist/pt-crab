Original 1: Does PT Work in the ED? Probably, Yea.

The Gist -This feasibility study assessed whether integrating a PT into the emergency department and having them treat and prescribe exercises could actually make an impact on the patients they saw. And it did! Yay! 30 ED patients with acute low back pain were evalled by an ED PT, given instructions and home exercises, then discharged home with instructions to report back after a week. When they came back, they demonstrated the exercises to the PT and reported on their at-home compliance. Patients reported performing home exercises 3 times between visits and 20 of the 27 who came back could show off all three exercises they were prescribed. This may be feasible after all.

Tell Me More -The goal of the study was just to see if patients would come back and if they’d do exercises in the meantime, it wasn’t about the physical progress they made. Though ED PT’s are common in the UK and Australia, they’re not yet popular here in the United States. This research group wants to change that, but it comes one step at a time. In this one, they did a 45 minute eval and discharged patients with exercises, with the promise of a $50 gift card if they came back. 27 of the 30 came back (hey, $50!), which isn’t too exciting, but 20 of those 27 could perform all three exercises they were prescribed and more reported actually doing the exercises when presented with an online survey. This is a small, basic study that will need to be fleshed out more in the future, but promising for the future of acute care PT.

UPDATE: PT in the ED is still gold

The Gist - The above was a pilot study and they haven’t finished their full piece yet but other authors have been working hard on this topic. So far, everything’s coming up physical therapy.

This piece in PTJ was a prospective observational study of ED-PT for acute LBP with 101 participants. 43 received ED-initiated PT and 58 got usual care. The median age was about 41 and 60% were women. People who got PT in the ED had greater improvements on their two outcome measures (ODI and PROMIS-PI) at 3 months and had lower use of “high-risk medications.”

Meanwhile, this piece in Academic Emergency Medicine did an RCT in Canada on PT in anyone 18-80 presenting with an MSK disorder in the ED. The intervention group got direct access to a PT prior to a physician in the ED and the control got usual care without a PT. They had 78 patients total with 40 in the PT group. Those who saw PTs first had significantly lower pain at 1 and 3 months, had fewer return visits to the ED, and less prescription medication was used at 1 month, through there were no differences at 3. There were also no differences for imaging and hospitalization rates. So some difference, not nothing, not incredible.

Original 2: If you PASS the PASS, you can go home.

The Gist -This study assessed the Postural Assessment Scale for Stroke (PASS) and its ability to determine acute care discharge recommendations. It was run on the neurology floor of a 782 bed (how specific!) acute care hospital with 153 participants. The PASS is a newish outcome measure for stroke that hasn’t been used consistently yet but is highly recommended by APTA StrokEDGE and this research team wanted to see if it could be helpful and quick in deciding discharge plans.

They taught their PTs how to pull it off, sent them into the wards, then collected data to see how the PASS fit with discharge recommendations. They didn’t use PASS to discharge, they just wanted to see how it lined up with their PT’s judgments. It was used within a median of 48 hours of admission and seemed to line up very well with discharge recommendations. Patients with a mean score of 31.8 (±2) were sent home with home PT, a 32.6 (±3) sent home with outpatient PT recommendations, 34.9 (±1) without PT recommendations, 15.7 (±8) to acute care rehab, and 9.5 (±11) to a SNF. This linear relationship and its ease of administration make it a good test to add to the battery of items in determining discharge recommendations.

Tell Me More - The PASS is a 12-item scale with each activity scored in a 0-3 scale. Activities include standing without support, sitting without support, standing and picking up a pencil from the floor, etc. It’s all balance based and a full breakdown is on the SRAlab site here.  There’s also a well-validated short form with just 5 items that the paper indicates could be useful as well. Overall, it assesses mobility and balance and has less of a floor and ceiling effect than the Berg Balance Scale, potentially making it more sensitive when used with severely and mildly impaired persons.

The authors are very specific to recommend it as a tool to guide discharge, but it does not predict performance on ADLs, so it can’t be used as the whole testing battery before sending the patient home. It’s strictly posture and balance. This paper also doesn’t indicate cutoff scores for discharge recommendations, that’s not what it was for. Rather, it shows that the PASS scales linearly with other indicators of discharge recommendations and asks for future studies to assess cutoffs. Keep an eye out for those.

UPDATE: What Else PASS Predicts

The Gist -That study above was pretty comprehensive and nothing exactly like it has been done since. What has been done is work on what else the PASS can predict.

This piece in PTJ found that the PASS at admission was predictive of dependence status at 3 months, with the score of 21 being the cutoff. Less than or equal to 21, likely to remain dependent. Greater than or equal to 22, likely to be independent. This held true even for those that were non-ambulatory on admission. To dig in a bit deeper, the trunk control elements of the PASS (numbers 1, 6, and 7, rolling to both sides and sitting without support) didn’t predict anything, only the 9 other elements. It goes deeper to reveal which specific items were predictive of independence to help guide rehab too, so check it out for more.

Lastly, this piece in the journal Nature (wow, I know) showed that a Swedish PASS (the SwePASS) score is associated with fear of falling at 6 months. Those with a PASS of less than or equal to 24 were 2.6x more afraid of falling than those with a score of 31 or higher at 6 months post stroke.
