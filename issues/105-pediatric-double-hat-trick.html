Power for CP

The Gist - We know a lot about how to treat CP, but what do we know about power-based training? These researchers saw a hole in the research market and dove in. They put 22 children with spastic CP who were classified as GMFCS I or II through a functional power training program where they performed running or walking exercises with loads applied to force them to move more powerfully. Exercises were 25 seconds each with rest periods of 30-50 seconds, 6-8 reps at a time. This was performed for 14 weeks in between two 14 week sessions of usual care.

For outcomes, they measured walking capacity and isometric muscle strength. The children all improved significantly in both of these categories and analysis from the researchers showed that increases in gastroc and hip abductor strength were most associated with improvements in sprint capacity, walking endurance, and walking speed.

I’ll close with the researchers’ assessment of their study: “The present results suggest that functional power training increases isometric and dynamic plantar flexor strength and walking capacity and it therewith may be a useful intervention to ameliorate the muscle weakness associated with botulinum toxin injections and serial casting.”

What do you do for Down syndrome?

The Gist -This survey-based poster from Beka Johnson interviewed 108 randomly selected practicing pediatric PTs to learn about what they think is a problem for people with Down syndrome and what they’re actually treating. There were some expected and unexpected mismatches. The expected mismatches were in areas like oral communication and cognitive and adaptive behavior. A few PTs saw these are primary problems, but no PTs reported primarily treating them, they’re not our thing. The unexpected mismatches were in joint stability, cardiopulmonary, and balance. 18% of PTs saw joint stability as a primary concern, but only 6% reported focusing on it in treatment. 6% pointed out cardiopulmonary concerns but just 1% treated it, and 10% talked about balance as a problem while just 7% treated it.

In these surveys, PTs couldn’t pick more than one thing, so it’s possible that all of these conditions are being treated by PTs but they’re just not primary. It’s an interesting rundown of what people are doing and thinking, so give it a look for yourself.

Hippotherapy helps. Short term at least.

The Gist -This meta-analysis looked into 7 studies on hippotherapy for CP and found that it’s probably helpful, at least in the short term. They modeled the information from the studies to see that there “was a statistically significant improvement with hippotherapy for lower-limb muscle spasticity in the short term in children with CP.” They didn’t detect significant differences between single and multiple sessions, though they didn’t have a lot to work with in their 7 studies. The researchers speculate that hippotherapy and therapeutic horseback riding cause muscle activation pattern changes due to the sensory stimulation experienced while performing the activity. For the short term nature of the effect, one study speculates that’s due to the motor learning experience that happens as one performs an activity more. A study that looked into 25 sessions of hippotherapy saw the effects drop off after 10 sessions were performed, speculatively due to the decreased muscle activation once one gets good at horseback riding.

Torticollis = GERD, Often

The Gist -This paper surveyed the medical records of 2,519 infants with torticollis to see how many of them also had GERD. Overall, they found GERD diagnoses present in 27.9% of individuals, while the general population presents with GERD about 23% of the time. Those with GERD were much more likely to have been born breech than the general population (8% vs 4%), and there were no associations with gender, history of multiparty, or age at first encounter.

The paper has a lot more information about their statistical methods, but that’s the meat of it in the paragraph above. One important note is that they emphasize a GERD diagnosis can be inaccurate and that not all GERD is caught, so they may not be capturing everything in this study.

What goes wrong with pediatric knees?

The Gist -This piece from Current Reviews in Musculoskeletal Medicine narrates common knee conditions seen in young athletes to “elucidate differences from the adult populations.” It’s a dive into what’s common and it’s very good reading. They split things into overuse and traumatic injuries, then define them, discuss the history and physical exam, then talk treatment and prognosis. This is written from the physician perspective, so some of the treatments aren’t too specific and others don’t fit within our scope of practice, but it’s still a great breakdown. I’d recommend giving it a scroll if this is a population you ever work with. Here’s the list of conditions:
Overuse:
- Osgood-Schlatter
- Sinding-Larsen-Johansson
- Osteochondritis Dissecans
- Plica Syndrome
- Discoid Meniscus
Traumatic:
- Patellar Instability/Dislocation
- Patellar Sleeve Fracture

Give the piece a look for a quick rundown of each.

Pediatric RTS Norms

The Gist -In our last piece today, we’re looking into a piece that is trying to establish pediatric norms for return to sport testing. Currently, athletes are usually judged solely by the limb symmetry index, but that isn’t perfect. To try to improve, these researchers from the Carolinas recruited 100 healthy people between the ages of 6 and 18 who had no prior ligamentous injuries to the lower extremity (amongst other things) and put them through a battery of return to sport tests.
They looked at the:
- Single-leg stork
- Single-leg stork on Bosu
- Single-leg squat
- Single-leg squat on Bosu
- Quadrant hop-clockwise
- Quadrant hop-counterclockwise
- Single-leg hop for distance
- 6-m timed hop
- Triple crossover hop for distance
Like I said, it was a battery.

Through it, they established some norms and information. For one, they saw that chronological age was a strong predictor of performance while sex only predicted performance on 2 of the 9 tests. They then put together some fantastic quantile fit plots that break down performance ranges by age. If you treat this population, definitely check this out. This is all early in the process, but it’s the best data we have so far.
