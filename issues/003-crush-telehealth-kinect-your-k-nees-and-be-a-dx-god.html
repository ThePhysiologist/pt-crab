Crush Telehealth, Kinect your K-nees, and Be a Dx God

This week in PT CRAB, we’re bringing new research to help you with Telehealth, tips and techniques on treating medial knee misalignment, and a fantastic database of diagnostics to up your diagnosis game. Seriously, don’t miss the last one, it’s great.

Let’s dive in!

Telehealth Gait Assessments and Cane Heights Work!

The Gist -

Good news for everyone currently seeing their patients over the phone or computer, the Tinetti POMA-G and cane height assessments are both valid and reliable on Telehealth! Researchers from Duke and Singapore collaborated on a paper recently published in PTJ checked bias, validity, and interrater reliability on the POMA-G and cane height assessments both in person and on video and showed that everything on video came up about as well as they do in the clinic. You need to take views from the side and head on to perform the assessments, but that’s easy enough to do.

I Want More -

Researchers checked interrater reliability and validity of cane height assessment and the POMA-G in three conditions: in the clinic, with normal speed video, and with slow motion video. In the video conditions, they used three different frame rates and bandwidths as well, checking to ensure that the tests were still reliable and valid under conditions of slow internet connections. And they were! The only real flaw was cane height assessment at low frame rates. ICC fell from .81 and .88 for the 30 and 15fps conditions down to just .66 for the lowest frame rate. Surprisingly, the POMA-G didn’t have these problems, and ICC was actually higher at lower frame rates.

In both cases, the bandwidth effects were high, lowering reliability massively as they fell from >700kbps to just 128 kbps. For the non IT-pros out there, 700Kbps is a pretty slow connection, about half of what you’d use to watch a low quality Netflix video and 10x slower than your cell phone’s connection. It’s the kind of speed that would make you reboot your router if it happened to you. 128kpbs is fast dial-up from back in the day. A crawl. What this data shows is that these assessments can work well, even with terrible internet connections. We don’t have too much data on other tele-assessments, and the researchers don’t generalize, but this should be empowering to try out some other scales when doing telehealth work.

Visual Feedback Helps Correct Medial Knee Displacement - Preventing PFP

The Gist - Another PFP-related study here, as researchers from App State and UVA published in the Journal of Athletic Training last month. They used the Microsoft Kinect, a motion-tracking device, to let patients see their knee motion in real time as they completed a single-leg drop vertical jump. (Here’s a video, I didn’t know what it was either…). The device showed them a model of their skeleton, then changed it’s color to green, yellow, or red based on their knee abduction angle. \<5° was green, >8° red, and yellow in between. The average subject reduced their knee abduction angle from about 8° to just under 5° after the intervention. Since landing with knee abducted causes less joint stability, this decrease could prevent injury.

I Want More - Previous researchers have theorized that theincreased Q angle in women leads to more noncontact ACL injuries than men, so this feedback method could reduce that, while staving off PFP. The researchers acknowledge that they are unsure if the effects they observed will last or if they’re temporary, but they did see good results after just one training session. They also observed reduced hip flexion and hip IR after the training sessions, indicating that landing kinematics changed beyond the knee.

Other previous research has shown that post-task feedback can modify movement patterns, these researchers just took it an extra step by applying the feedback during the test. Most clinics lack a Kinect, but the use of a mirror with some angled lines drawn on it in dry erase could simulate the effects of the Kinect feedback. If you want the full value of the study, Kinect’s are inexpensive too. Multiple studies have shown that feedback (realtime and traditional) does alter lower extremity movement patterns, so it’s certainly a technique of value that any clinic could use.

One Database of Diagnostic Tests to Rule Them All

The Gist - The Journal of Physiotherapy and the Australian Physiotherapy Association recently announced a fantastic new resource for PTs. It’s DiTA, the Database of Diagnostic Test Accuracy. They’ve put together a compendium of study types and their stats to help us all be better clinicians. Did you forget the sensitivity and specificity of Ayer’s test? Want more assessments to perform on the ankle? DiTA has you covered. You can search for details on a specific test or pick a body part to browse tests about. It’s a great refresher and learning tool that everyone should consider adding to their practice.

I Want More - I don’t really have anything else to say about DiTA except that it’s awesome and useful and free and accessible right here. Seriously, stop reading this and go there now.
