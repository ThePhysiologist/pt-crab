Run Run As Fast As You Can

This week in PT Crab we’re doing a 3/4 running issue, with three articles about it and one that’s not. I’ll bet you did the math on that yourself already though. It’s the time of year where people lace up their shoes and head out the door. Only to (all too often) head back in the door 40 minutes later with shin splints and pain in their arches. We’d like to prevent that. So let’s get started.

Let’s dive in!

Strong Foot, Good Run

The Gist - The feet are often overlooked in PT care, even with runners where you think they’d be top of mind though still bottom of body. This proof of concept trial wanted to see what a foot strengthening program would do to foot muscle morphology and running mechanics. They single blinded it, putting 28 folks into two groups, one with the intervention and one for control. The intervention was an 8 week foot strengthening program once a week with a PT and thrice a week at home. The control group were given a full body stretching routine to do before or after each run.

Outcome-wise, they looked into isometric foot strength, cross-sectional area and volume, stiffness, foot function scores, and propulsive impulses during running. These were tested at the beginning of the study and after 8 weeks. They measured a lot of stuff, but not much stuff changed, somewhat surprisingly. Unsurprisingly, cross sectional area and propulsive ability both increased, but most other outcome measures did not.

Tell Me More -The surprising outcomes are those that didn’t come through, namely hallux and toe muscle strength didn’t increase according to the way they measured them. Even so, the propulsive forces from the feet did increase, showing that the exercise program can change running dynamics if not measurable toe strength. Why do we care at all? Because this directly shows how intrinsic foot strengthening can change running mechanics. And yes, it seems obvious, I know, but we have to test this stuff to make sure.

What exercises did they do? Gonna have to read the paper for those folks.

Before we close though, I want to emphasize that this was a small, single-blind, pilot study. Which means, technically, we don’t know anything after it. We just have some hints.

More Pain = More PT. More PT = More Pain. O.o

The Gist -This is a dense paper from a group of researchers who love themselves some clinimetrics (and, let’s be honest, we all do, right? Right? Oh, it’s just me…). A bundle of researchers from Virginia Commonwealth University looked at the association between the number of PT visits and physical function after TKA and their results surprise.

In brief, for the first two months, there was no association between the number of PT visits and pain + disability. After that though, things change. People who went to PT more in months 2 through 6 had higher pain scores at 6 months, and they went to PT more in months 6 - 12. At 12 months, these people still had more pain than those who went less in the first few months. How does this affect your practice? Not a lot, directly. It introduces something to think about as these patients drag on 6 or 8 months after knee surgery. Check out the block quote from the researchers below for more.

Tell Me More -The researchers grabbed these patient cases out of a huge trial, called KASTPain, that was published a couple of years ago. That trial took people who catastrophize about pain and split them into three arms post TKA: pain coping skills training, arthritis education, and usual care. They didn’t limit who could go to PT, so the researchers had a big bundle of heterogenous data to pull from. They ended up with 326 participants with an average age of about 63. These were all patients who catastrophize about pain, so keep that in mind when assessing the findings. That’s probably the major limitation of the study, though the researchers point out that their overall recoveries were similar to people without pain catastrophization. I’ll leave you with this, from the authors, about what this means for us moving forward:

The challenge for physical therapists is that greater utilization also leads to greater costs, and, given that physical therapy is already known to account for the majority of medical costs during the year following KA it is likely to be a target for future cost reductions. Therefore, future research should focus on methods for enhancing efficiency of care, particularly for those patients with persistent pain, as these patients are likely the highest utilizers of physical therapy following KA and consequently the most costly.
Basically, we’re not there yet on how to treat these people. But keep your eyes open for them and consider novel ideas.

What Running Coaches Think About Injuries

The Gist -Runners work in packs. We’ve all seen it. Sure, there’s the occasional loan wolf stalking the wild miles in solo silence, but these urban predators tend to move in free flowing pack organizations. Scientists aren’t yet sure why, but research is happening. Some of that research appears here, where a couple of Brits asked 100 running coaches and group leaders that they think about prehab and injury prevention strategies in general.

Good news! Most coaches (70%) do a warmup with their runners (usually dynamic) and also most coaches (53%) do a cool down. Yay! 40% give advice on recovery strategies sometimes and 11% give it always too. On the downside, most of the recovery strategies were stretching (not backed by research), and resting (somewhat backed by research), while only a bit of it included strength, physiotherapy, hydration, or sleep. A bit even included breathing(?!). I hope everyone always breathes, even if it’s not a recovery strategy.

On the injury prevention side, half of coaches said they advise about footwear (something on which the research is very murky) while only 9 advise about running form or technique (something on which the research is very clear).

Tell Me More -You may be again asking, why does this matter? Because these coaches and running group leaders are primary sources for your runners’ information about their bodies. It’s either them or Running Magazine. Seriously. So what they think matters. Big time. And it appears that what they think isn’t wrong, but it isn’t really right either.

The paper goes into much greater detail about where coaches get their information, how strongly they believe in it, and more, so if this is something that interests you, definitely check it out. And for you independent clinicians out there, these folks may really appreciate your professional advice on how to keep their runners fit and healthy. Just a small marketing idea (hint).

Here’s a bit more info about where they are

Almost all coaches/RGLs said they would find it beneficial as a Coach/RGL to learn more about prehab/injury prevention and thought it feasible within their coaching/running session to spend time addressing prehab… However, Coaches/RGL’s reported inconsistent and conflicting current advice on prehab, thus more direction is needed to understand what prehab exercises are most effective, when and how they could be incorporated in training, and why they are important for injury prevention.

Run after hip arthroscopy? Yupp.

The Gist -Can you return to running after arthroscopic hip surgery.

Yes.

Tell Me More -I’m kidding, let’s go back to the gist.

The Gist -This team of physicians and PTs wanted to come up with some kind of plan and protocol for returning to running after hip arthroscopy, so they did. And then they wrote about it. They used the program for 3 years with 400 patients who had undergone hip labral repair, acetabular rim resection, and/or osteochondroplasty and it appears to work well. They recommend starting the program 3 months after surgery for most patients and tell them to progress gradually, avoid hills, start on a treadmill first, recovery properly, and cross train. Then they give them a plan full of strengthening exercises, simple plyometrics, and recommendations on when/how much to run. Full details of what that looks like are in the paper. The basic structure is below.

Tell Me More -Again, for details, see the paper. The basic structure is a walking program that gets people up to 30 minutes at a brisk pace, then a quick response/plyometric program that progresses up to 500 foot contacts in a session (and videos of the exercises are linked to in the paper. How cool!). After that, it’s on to a walk/jog program and a very slow return to distance running as long as they can run without pain and don’t have it 48 hours later. As part of this, they establish a baseline distance to work from with specific guidelines on what to do each day relative to that baseline. It’s a well thought out program that’s easy to follow, so consider checking it out and adding it to your arsenal for your runners.

I’ll leave you with the researchers’ final words of warning: “The novel program proposed here has been used with success in our institution; however, it has not been validated with long-term outcomes, and therefore should be treated as a guideline that can be altered according to individual needs.” So be careful. But you already knew that.
