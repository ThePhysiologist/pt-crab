Issue 160

Knees and Toes

Let’s talk about a topic that’s close to my heart today, because it’s going on inside my body. And that’s patellofemoral pain. My right patella just plain don’t move straight. And it’s a problem. Which got me thinking about articles along these lines. I shouldn’t try to treat this all myself, but you know I’m going to. My meandering research for ideas connected with another of my favorite topics, and one we’ve covered recently, feet. So this week we’re looking into the connection between feet and knees, and I don’t mean the tibia. So we’re going to talk about foot orthoses, foot mobility, and foot/ankle mechanics and the affects they each have on patellofemoral pain. And hopefully I’ll learn something. And you will too.

Let’s dive in!

First, Orthoses

The Gist -To be specific, foot orthoses versus hip control, which is generally my go-to for PFP and I imagine it’s yours too. The researchers wanted to answer a complex question, whether those with patellofemoral pain and greater pronation benefited from foot orthoses more than hip exercises. And they didn’t. Foot mobility had no effect on response to exercise or orthosis, but it’s not all bad. It’s actually quite interesting. Across the entire group of 192 participants who followed up at 12 weeks, everyone improved about equally. On average, at least. All groups: flexible foot, stiff foot, hip exercise, and foot orthosis, improved equally. But I’m ahead of myself, so let’s remind a bit.

They measured every participants’ foot pronation via weight-bearing and non weight-bearing measurements o the width of the participant’s mid foot. 11mm was the cutoff between high and low mobility. They then split everyone into two groups, foot orthosis and hip exercise. Details of both interventions in the next section. Everyone was treated by PTs blinded to the details of the study and had multiple exercise sessions. The primary outcome measure was the Global Rate of Change scale (GROC) and success was defined as someone feeling “much better”, or “better” on the GROC. By that metric, as 12 weeks, 46/92 people in the hip exercise group succeeded and 48/100 people in the foot orthosis group succeeded. Pretty much the same (50% vs. 48%).

Tell Me More -Now, details. The actual pursuit of the study was to see if people with more mobile feet improved more from foot orthoses than those with less mobile feet, and, like I said, they didn’t. Secondarily though, we did learn that both interventions are pretty good, like I said above. Now on to something new.

The hip exercises were laid out in detail in another study, (The Foot Orthoses versus Hip eXercises (FOHX) trial for patellofemoral pain: a protocol for a randomized clinical trial to determine if foot mobility is associated with better outcomes from foot orthoses)but were pretty much what you would expect. They targeted the posterolateral hip muscles that we go after for knee control. These were one-on-one with 3 sessions per week for 4 weeks with each exercise shooting for a max of 10 reps and RPE of 5-7/10. The participants weren’t told whether or not to continue the exercises after the four weeks.

Over on the foot side, they used pre-fab Vionics orthoses, one set to go in their main shoes and another that was a pair of sandals so they could wear them more often. PTs fit them to each participant and then gave them a 2x/day HEP consisting of calf stretches and anti-pronation foot exercises. They got 6 sessions over the course of 6 weeks and also weren’t told whether or not to continue the exercises after the 6 weeks were up.

Everyone was asked to followup at 12 weeks and most did so (192/216).

There were secondary outcome measures that I didn’t get into, but I do want to focus on the primary again for a second, the GROC. It’s a 7 point scale that has someone score their problem as much better, better, a little better, no change, a little worse, worse, or much worse.

Alright, I’m out.

Paper?Got it. 

But what do orthoses do?

The Gist -This article, from the journal Gait and Posture, wanted to know what effects orthoses had on lower limb biomechanics, pain, and confidence in people with patellofemoral arthritis. It was, as you may imagine, a pretty simple study to pull off. They recruited 21 participants with a mean age of 58 who all had patellofemoral OA, did some motion capture, gave them foot insets, and did motion capture again. To be thorough, they did motion capture in pre-fab arch-contouring orthoses and flat shoe inserts, all of which were fit to them by a podiatrist prior to the session. The countered ones were Vasyli orthoses with in-built arch support and a 6° varus wedge.

They captured motion in level gait, up stairs, and down stairs with six trials of each condition to see what would change. The main differences between the conditions on flat ground were peak ankle dorsiflexion angle, and peak ankle dorsiflexion moment. They saw no significant effects during ascent, but had similar effects to level walking on descent.

There wasn’t a difference between orthoses and none when it came to pain scores or knee confidence scores, just the biomechanics stuff.

Tell Me More -To dig a little deeper into the biomechanics, the design of these inserts promote more plantarflexion in stance, potentially influencing foot pronation as well. The researchers didn’t see any significant kinematic differences at other joints, things stayed just about the same. Which like, is weird, I know. I would think that you’d see differences up the chain if you did down the chain, but nope! That’s mostly because the differences were pretty darn small.

That plays in to some theories that orthoses don’t make a big difference, because our bodies make minute adaptations to return to the kinematic patterns they’re used to. This paper doesn’t go into that, so who knows. But I’m going to show you the paper anyway.

Paper? Yupps.

If you have arthritis, what are your feet like?

The Gist -We’ll close out by looking closely at feet, again. We’re doing it via the FPI, foot posture index, and the mid foot mobility measurement you may remember from earlier in this edition. The researchers in this paper, published in the Journal of Foot and Ankle Research, wanted to see what the feet of people who had patellofemoral arthritis had in common.

They recruited 188 people with patellofemoral OA who were already in a trial about foot orthoses (hmmm, familiar) and took a bunch of measurements. Weightbearing dorsiflexion ROM, foot posture index, mid foot mobility, the number of repeated single step-ups they could do before the onset of knee pain, the number of double-leg sit to stands they could do before knee pain, the severity of knee pain over the last week, the anterior knee pain scale, and the knee injury and osteoarthritis outcome scale. Phew.

The people who more quickly had pain provocation in the step-up and sit to stand tasks also had lower weightbearing dorsiflexion ROM, and a higher foot posture index score, indicating a more pronated foot posture. Arch height and mid foot width weren’t associated with the pain provocation tests.

Tell Me More -Non of the results here are surprising but, as always, it’s nice to have some data to go alongside our assumptions. We also don’t know if improving these deficiencies would improve the condition, cuz that’s how research works. But we know that having more pronated feet at rest in stance and having lower weightbearing ankle dorsiflexion both are associated with patellofemoral arthritis. As someone who may already have it, I’m going to start some stretching and strengthening.

All of these relationships were of a small magnitude, so we’re in a constant state of shrug here too. Maybe there’s an association, maybe there’s not. Technically it’s significant. Clinically significant though? We’ll have to wait and see.

Paper?Gots it. 

And that’s our week! I’ll avoid feet for a little while but I hope you didn’t mind my deviation down the kinetic chain.
