When to start vestibular rehab post-concussion? Now is good.

The Gist -First off, if you’re not a vestibular therapist, think carefully about your ability to treat it. As someone who was mis-treated at the hands of a clinician who didn’t know what they were doing, I’m very sensitive to this. Second off, if you do vestibular rehab, do indeed start early. This pilot study of 23 young patients (average age 16.14) tells us that beginning vestibular rehab in under 30 days post-concussion allows symptom resolution and return to play significantly earlier.

This was a retrospective study where they looked into the info of two groups who had sustained sport-related concussions. The group that started vestibular PT more than 30 days post concussion (an average of 74 days later with a range of 38 to 125) returned to sport an average of 110 days after the concussion. The groups that started early (an average of 22 days post concussion with a range of 17 to 28) returned to sport at 31 days on average.

Tell Me More -This is a pilot study and the ranges on the late therapy folks are very high, as you can see, so we can’t take too much away from this. What we can say is that in this group of young people, early vestibular therapy got them back faster. Exactly how much faster relative to days since concussion remains to be seen. The short study has a bit more information inside as well.

Days to symptom resolution for the early folks was just 54 (range 27-91) while it averaged 122 (range 71-223) for the late ones. Interestingly, the return to learn days for these groups were inverted, with the late therapy kids returning at 12 days on average (3.5-26.5) and the earlys returning at 18 (8-21). Why did some people start so late even though they reported to the concussion clinic at about the same time (12ish days)? The researchers think it may be due to practical barriers and screening for subtypes. Check out the paper for details.

Respiratory PT is Cost-Effective for COPD. In Australia.

The Gist -I say in Australia for two reasons 1) they use different money over there in the upside-down and B) they assess healthcare costs differently than we do here in also upside-down as far as healthcare is concerned land.

This was a systematic review that looked into 11 studies that performed respiratory PT interventions to improve exercise capacity and physical activity in people with COPD. There were multiple interventions across the studies, including airway clearance techniques, pulmonary rehabilitation (home-based and centre-based), and more. Content, timing, duration, etc. varied study by study, from a minimum of 7 days to a max of 24 months in duration and a minimum of 1 session per 3 months to 3x/wk for timing. Outcome measures also varied and I’m not going to get into them here. What I will say is that “Of the 11 included studies, four studies were interpreted to be cost-effective while two were not. In five studies, the cost-effectiveness was uncertain or dependent on the willingness to pay.” This cost-effectiveness is based on assessments of cost per unit quality of life gained (which is a weird but understandable metric and ranged from 959.99 euros to 38,000) and QALYSs, quality adjusted life years.

Tell Me More -No. Cost quantification is complicated but important. If you’re interested in more details, you’ll have to check out the paper on this one. It is well written and contains an effective compendium of studies so if you’re interested in respiratory therapy you will learn from it and its citations. But I can’t dig any more here.

Neural Mobilization works! For some stuff.

The Gist -Remember last week when I wrote about neural mobilization from the Canadian Journal of Physiotherapy? Maybe it was a few weeks ago, who knows. Anyway, it got me interested in neural mobilization so I went back a few years to a JOSPT article from 2017. They did a systematic review and meta-analysis of neural mobilization for neuromusculoskeletal conditions (took me three tries to spell that!) to find that it appears to be useful for people with chronic low back pain and chronic neck-arm pain, but not carpal tunnel or lateral epicondylalgia.

They reviewed 21 studies and meta-analyzed 19 to get those results, more details of which are below. These neural mobilizations differ from the Canadian ones, these are what we would commonly call nerve glides. Things like the slump test, cervical lateral glides, straight leg raise, etc. This is a great roundup of studies and I can’t get through them all, but here are some quick highlights.

Slump and SLR had significant effects on pain intensity (-2 on 0-10 scale) and disability (-11 on Oswestry) in people with chronic nerve related low back pain compared to exercises or lumbar mobilization.

Cervical lateral glides reduced nerve-related neck and arm pain by 2 points on 0 - 10 compared to ultrasound, no treatment, and advice only, while sliding and tensioning exercises improved pain when compared to exercise and traction.

Tell Me More -Read the paper. But if you can’t and you’re here for a summary (duh), let’s dig more into what didn’t work. Carpal tunnel syndrome had 12 studies that looked into it, and it doesn’t seem to decrease pain or increase function significantly when compared to splint, splint and ultrasound, sham NM, advice, paraffin, and many other comparison treatments. There were positive signs like decreased median nerve latency and edema, but no outcome measure significant differences.

Lastly, three studies assessed lateral epicondylalgia and showed mixed results and mixed implementation. It’s possible that this does work for this condition, but we don’t have the data to show it yet. The most effective study used cervical lateral glides, jsyk.

The other conditions we don’t have enough data on yet are tarsal tunnel syndrome, plantar heel pain, cubital tunnel syndrome, and post-lumbar surgery. They each had one study included in the paper, and SLR showed positive results for plantar heel pain, but we don’t have enough data on all of them to make a judgment yet. Just gotta wait and see.

Check out the paper for more, it’s one of the better written meta-analyses.
