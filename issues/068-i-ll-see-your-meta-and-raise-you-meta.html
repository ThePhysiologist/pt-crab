Let’s dive in!

Don’t be afraid. Don’t be very afraid.

The Gist -This piece from the Journal of Arthroplasty makes the argument that fear is not a good thing. I don’t think any of us thought that, but they put numbers to it, which makes it even better. Specifically, they looked at 463 total joint arthroplasty patients (hip and knee) to see how anxiety, depression, and pain catastrophization scores affected their recoveries. They found that those with any of these three conditions had worse pre-op and post-op pain and function scores. Fortunately, they did improve post-operatively, and they even improved slightly more than people without these conditions, but they never got to functional levels as high as those who weren’t anxious, depressed, or catastrophizing. In these patients, objective function wasn’t worse than non-depressed etc. patients, but subjective function was, and this lowered their eventual objective function at the end of rehab. Quite interesting I’d say.

Tell Me More -There are interventions that PTs can do for these things, such as cognitive behavioral therapy techniques and reducing fear-avoidance behavior, and the way that we talk to our patients can definitely help as well. The present literature doesn’t go into that, but we’ll be covering it in the near future. There’s always referral to mental health providers too, don’t forget! In other notes, the outcome measures used in this study were the Harris Hip and Knee Society Scores. To measure anxiety, depression, and pain catastrophization, they used the Hospital Anxiety and depression scale and the Pain Catastrophization Scale. And for some numbers, the differences a year later weren’t huge, but they were there. Pain was 1.0 greater for the catastrophizers (on 0-10), and HHS/KSS were 1.3 less. Technically significant but all smaller than MCIDs for the scales involved, jsyk.

SIJ Tests. Probably not worth the time.

The Gist -JOSPT coming in hot once again with this systematic review and meta-analysis of pain provocation test clusters for detecting SIJ pain. The authors went through thousands of articles to find 5 that are includable. These 5 had samples sizes from 34-150 patients, aged averagely from 42 to 58.3 years, and mostly female (67-88%). They used the “distraction test, thigh thrust test, Gaenslen test, compression test, sacral thrust test, the flexion, abduction, external rotation test, sacral distraction test, lateral compresion test, Patrick test, yeoman test, Newton test, and the ipsilateral Gaenslen test” in a variety of clusters and the authors duly dug into their data to figure out what worked. Truth be told, nothing did very well.

Pooling them all together gave a positive likelihood ratio of 2.13 and negative of 0.33. Not very powerful. For an idea of how little power that is, we need to use likelihood ratios properly. LRs answer this question: “What is the chance that the clinical condition (SIJ pain) will be present or absent in the context of a positive or negative test result?” Based on the prevailing prevalence in that population. So before using a test and LR, you need to know a prevalence. For their study, the authors tested at prevalences of 20 and 30%. At 20%, a positive test gives a 35% posttest probability and negative is 8%. At 30%, these go to 48% and 12%. BUT real-world prevalence could be as low as 2%, making those values 4% for positive tests and 1% for negative. That’s not much power.

Tell Me More -Well, I already told you a lot, but I can continue a bit. On top of all that, the applied reference standards used in the studies (the “gold standard” to see if a test was accurate) “was problematic in all studies” according to the authors. This is not good. Overall, this fantastically written qualitative review tells us that SIJ clusters just may not be very accurate or useful in the real world. This is some of the most readable statistical work I’ve seen in a paper, it’s just sad that it leads to such a conclusion.

How do you measure satisfaction with PT? ¯_(ツ)/¯

The Gist -An important part of PT treatment is understanding patient satisfaction. An important part of understanding satisfaction is looking into the surveys that measure it. And it turns out that they don’t. At least, not well. This systematic review covered nine different “instruments specifically designed to assess satisfaction with physical therapy care” these were the
- Rehabilitation Services Satisfaction Survey
- Physical Therapy Outpatient Satisfaction Survey
- Chest Physiotherapy Satisfaction Survey
- Physical Therapy Patient Satisfaction Questionnaire
- Patient Satisfaction with Physical Therapy
- MedRisk
- Treatment Outcome Satisfaction Questionnaire
- User Attention Questionnaire
- Patient Satisfaction With Outpatient Physiotherapy Questionnaire

Who knew there were that many? Wow.
For the highlights, cross-cultural validity and measurement error were both problems across the questionnaires, as was sensitivity to change. MedRisk was the most widely validated and evaluates the most psychometric properties it was “found to be the most robust instrument.”

Tell Me More -The problem with many of these, according to the researchers, is that they were modified from different fields to enter physical therapy, making them less valid for this service. And even though MedRisk looks like “the best” the researchers are quick to point out that “some other measuring instruments have greater internal consistency and greater structural validity,” and they “recommend that your choice be justified for use as a tool according to the psychometric properties shown in this systematic review.” Unfortunately, the review doesn’t do a great job looking into those psychometric properties, so there’s not really a solution here. There is a great table in the article that could help you choose which properties to use, so give that a look if you’re trying to decide. Few tools are better than “Fair” or “Poor” in their COSMIN scores, but a couple (like Patient Satisfaction with Physical Therapy) do okay there.

Paper?Sure. Check out that table, it’s where the real goodies are. Open access for APTA members here.

You Can Reduce Pain Just By Saying The Right Thing

The Gist -Another piece of clickbait, I know, but it’s based on a real paper, promise! This narrative review from researchers in Chile and Canada describes how verbal suggestion can help manage MSK pain. It’s really cool!

They explain the neurobiology of verbal suggestion and the particulars of what therapists can say to reduce pain. We’ll focus on the latter here (but the former is really good and you should read the whole paper to see it). The review discovered that positive verbal suggestion and high levels of empathy significantly favor the analgesic effect, while neutral communication can make it worse. The magnitude of these changes is controversial, but the effect seems to be there across the studies. Additionally, specific information about how the intervention will help the patient has been demonstrated to make a difference in how effective it is. So sell yourself people!

Tell Me More -Before you think it, this study is not saying that you should trick your patients. Rather, it points out how important word choice is in therapy and provides a huge reference list to that effect, one that I will certainly be reading. The major takeaway is the value of verbal suggestion about the potential for gain through a therapeutic method. There’s promising research that talking about interventions more positively leads to better outcomes.

There are some limitations to the research that the group was able to pull from. Some of the studies included had methodological limitations, like lack of a control group or heterogeneity of the subjects and treatments. This is an emerging field of study and it’s not quite there yet. I would recommend checking out the bibliography to find individual studies that you can use to guide your practice.
