Fearful Contractions in ACLR

The Gist - If you’re afraid, according to the Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia, you’re probably landing differently after your ACLR. At least, if you’re one of these 38 patients who had an ACLR within the last two years. They were compared with 39 healthy controls and had all returned to physical activity and felt confident performing hop tests prior to this study. The researchers used motion capture and EMG to compare the kinematics and muscle activation of the three groups (high fear, low fear, and control) and see just what a difference kinesiophobia made. And there were indeed differences.

At initial contact, trunk, knee, and hip flexion were significantly different between all three groups, with high fear having more trunk flexion than the control group and both high and low fears having more hip and knee flexion. Muscularly, the high fear group had higher biceps femoris activation and higher anterior-posterior co-contraction index scores (i.e. they had more co-contraction around the joint).

In addition to the objective data, they took outcome measures as well, where they saw no differences between the high and low fear groups for the Tegner Activity scale, the International Physical Activity Scale, and the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score. Though there weren’t differences between the fearful groups, they did score lower than control on all except the Knee Injury and OA scale.

Tell Me More -Equally as interesting as what the researchers saw is what they didn’t see. They had expected to see fear-related differences during the pre-activation phase of the test. Instead, all increased forces were reactive, not proactive. The overall theory is that this increased co-contraction and biceps femoris activation leads to a stiffer joint and less strain on the graft, good things. But they can also lead to increased tibiofemoral compression and knee joint degeneration, bad things.

The lack of difference between the fearful groups in the kinematic variables is notable as well. Both groups tended to increase hip and knee joint flexion, resulting in a lower center of mass. The researchers say that increased knee flexion could lead to “smaller patellar tendon insertion angles, greater hamstrings insertion angles, lower anterior tibial shear force, and lower tibiofemoral peak compression forces, all of which are considered to decrease ACL strain,” so that’s quite a nice adaptation.

The discussion of limitations in this paper and the overall discussion section are very illuminating and easy to read, so if you like ACLR content, do check it out.

Humanity and PT

The Gist -In a perspective piece that digs into how social adversity affects our genes, our rehab, and our whole selves, N. Snyder-Mackler and L. Snyder-Mackler discuss how “social isolation, discrimination, or low social status,” are types of social adversity that negatively impact health. We all know about the rising inequality and social adversity in our society, so they look at the mechanisms of how these affect our health. The main focus is the hypothalamic-pituitary-axis that secretes glucocorticoids and “shunts energy from noncritical functions (like reproduction and immunity) while mobilizing energy to respond to the acute stressor.” Under chronic stimulation, the HPA-axis develops glucocorticoid resistance, losing negative feedback controls and not turning itself off when required. Bad news.

We’ve talked recently about my recent chronic stress (which is getting better, btw, yay!) and guess who was sick a couple of weeks ago?

Not with Covid, thankfully, but I’m sure the immune dysfunction associated with HPA problems is at least partially to blame.

The authors go on to discuss how social adversity accelerates aging, molecularly alters many organs, leads to cellular senescence, and causes epigenetic changes. All bad news. But what about rehab? Read on.

Tell Me More -The researchers discuss how biological embedding of physical stressors can modify prognosis and how the current social environment can greatly change one’s experience with rehab. This is something we innately know as PTs and the writers argue that we should take it further by building more supportive and predictable environments in PT and helping patients build these at home.

Similar to the environmental responsibilities article from a few weeks ago, this paper is scant on details as to what specific actions we should take in treatment. It seems to be designed more as a general knowledge piece to ensure that this information is in our heads when meeting with our patients. So do consider it, understand the holistic environment in which your patients live, and think of how you can decrease their social adversity in future.

Paper? -Open access here, also in PTJ.

Are you afraid of this? How about this?

The Gist -Normally, showing people pictures and asking if they’re afraid of what they see is the domain of psychopaths and fMRI researchers (I’m sure there’s a bit of Venn Diagram crossover there as well), but this research was about fear avoidance in patients with shoulder pain and it’s quite interesting. The writers are trying to develop a new scale to see what activities people with shoulder pain tend to avoid IRL, so they had 120 healthcare professionals and 50 patients review 107 preselected photos to see which they thought were valid to include. Eventually, that bundle was whittled down to 15 photos in three domains: free movement, high effort, and self-care.

The whole thing is based on similar scales: the Pictorial Fear of Activity Scale and the Photograph Series of Daily Activities, both of which are for back pain (cervical and lumbar, respectively). This one is designed for shoulder pain and is apparently the first of its kind. And it’s not yet usable. So far, they’ve established content validity, structural validity, and internal consistency, but not usefulness. Yet.

Tell Me More -Unless you’re really interested in how they determine structural validity, there’s not a lot more here. And I’m not really interested in that, so I’m not going to write about it. What I will do instead is show you what new scale could look like in the relatively near future.

Only time (and intense research) will tell if we’ll have this in clinics soon, but it’s an interesting idea building on prior work on the back, and I’d definitely recommend looking into those other photographic scales for lumbar and cervical spine.

Paper?But of course. Also open access. Also PTJ. Also here.

Please Stand So Close To Me

The Gist -If you’re doing the FGA for community-dwelling older adults, does contact guarding influence the scores? Nope.

Tell Me More -23 community-dwelling older adults (74 years average age) were given trials on the FGA, one with contact guard and the other with standby. They were video recorded and two PTs blinded to the purpose of the study scored them. After scoring, there was no significant difference between the scores on the two trials, with a P value of 0.882. Yay! So it looks like you may be able to contact guard the FGA.

And I know what you’re saying, aren’t all P values supposed to be 0.05. I learned 0.05! 0.05! 0.05!!. First off, calm down. And secondly: No. Especially no for this study. There’s a lot of statistical controversy about the use of 0.05 as the cutoff score, but that’s for another post (and really, another site). But in this study, they were ensuring that there weren’t significant differences between the two. Getting a p of 0.05 (or even 0.4) would’ve shown a significant difference, something that we don’t want.

Want even more details? Then read the paper folks. This one is well-written, clear, and important. And if you like Olds, make sure you check out the Journal of Geriatric Physical Therapy in general. Their older stuff is open access. And admittance to the Geriatric section (and thus access to the journal) is currently free for students.
