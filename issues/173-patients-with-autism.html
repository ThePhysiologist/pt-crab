Issue 173

Patients with Autism

n.b. I’m writing this edition in a bit more of a narrative format. Let me know what you think after reading it by replying, I’m curious to learn!

Working with people who have autism is a challenging topic that isn’t covered well in most PT schools. That’s even while one in 36 children were identified with autism spectrum disorder in 2020 (CDC, 2020). That means that almost 3% of the population could be living with autism, but we still don’t know much about working with these patients appropriately. People with autism are at increased risk of mortality for all categories of disease except infection, are 25% more likely to visit a GP, and are almost 3x more likely to be hospitalized than age-matched general population controls (Weiss, 2018). Some speculate that they go to more appointments because they have difficulty finding providers who have knowledge about autism (Zerbo, 2015).

People with autism are usually less happy with their medical providers than a general population. They also have lower healthcare self-efficacy and more unmet needs (Nicolaidis, 2013). This is probably because communication with healthcare providers can be difficult for those with autism, especially since few healthcare providers are well-trained to communicate with autistic people. So how do we get better?

All of this information is either directly from or branching off of our main paper today from the Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, A Systematic Review of What Barriers and Facilitators Prevent and Enable Physical Healthcare Services Access for Autistic Adults. Let’s dive into it more deeply.

The Barriers

This systematic review looked at 6 studies, two quantitative, two qualitative, and two mixed-method. Barriers were legion and broadly fit into three categories, personal, provider, and systemic. We are most closely attached to provider so that’s going to be the focus with some personal sprinkled in. That includes things like:
- Communication difficulty
- Problems with over-stimulation
- Discomfort with touch
- Atypical discussions or experiences of pain
- Waiting room anxiety
- Lack of provider understanding of autism
- Lack of trust in providers
- Slow processing speed
- Difficulty understanding spoken instructions
- Lack of consistency

These things together can cause a bad cycle. As an example, waiting rooms can be stressful for people with autism because of how waiting can cause anxiety and the unpredictable environment that waiting rooms can be. People with autism generally like clear instructions and environments and waiting rooms are not that. This stress can lead to sensory sensitivity, which leads to overstimulation, making concentration and social interaction more difficult. This causes increased fear in social interactions, making it more difficult to talk to their provider. Just waiting room stress can directly contribute to poorer healthcare outcomes for these people. It may already be clear how you can fix that, and more is below.

One study found that 75% of participants had three or more barriers to healthcare access (Vogan, 2017). Sensory sensitivities were a prominent barrier, but slow processing speed, difficulty with executive functioning, stress about remembering elements of the visit, and difficulty with verbal communication were also elements as well. As PTs, we do a lot of verbal communication (I, for one, do too much), and that can be tough for those who have trouble processing it. We’ll continue with facilitators below as well, but it’s a good time to mention that paper versions of your patient education could be really valuable here so that those with autism aren’t stressed about keeping up with every word.

The Facilitators

So how can we make things better? There are some easy ways to implement and some harder ones.

On the easy side, minimizing sensory overload can go first. This can mean:
- Removing smelly cleaning agents
- Minimizing stimulation in the waiting room by removing music or television
- Turning down lighting in offices and waiting rooms
- Having a cordoned section in a waiting room that’s quieter
- Allowing people with autism to wait outside or in their cars and calling them when it’s time to come in
- Working with them in treatment rooms rather than the gym space
Taking a thoughtful approach to your clinic space and calming it all down could make a big difference in these people’s lives. And if you can’t do that, just treating them in a more private area or letting them wait outside could be a big help too.

Some tougher areas to change include settling autistic people’s fear of stigmatization, understanding the diversity of autism’s presentation, taking processing speed and memorization difficulty into account, and providing more clear instructions. Fortunately, there are tools for that! One big tool is covered in this paper, the Autism Healthcare Accommodations Toolkit (AHAT). It’s part of the AASPIRE Healthcare Toolkit and allows people with autism to go through a survey that makes a report at the end. The report can be provided to a healthcare provider to better inform them about the patient’s specific needs and deficits to improve the healthcare interaction. Wrapping up

If this interests you, I highly recommend checking out more in the AASPIRE Healthcare Toolkit. It’s a fantastic resource. They have a lot more for healthcare providers and quite a bit for patients and their supporters as well. It’s not specifically designed for PTs, but it is useful for us too. And can help us overcome another barrier, lack of understanding about the condition.

Autism is complicated. I didn’t really explain anything about the condition here because there’s too much to go into. It’s very broad. And very common. That’s why tools like the AHAT are nice. They let you understand the patient’s individual needs and provide the care that’s best for this one person. And even if you don’t have the AHAT, active listening and asking patients about their individual needs is a perfect first step. If you find out that a patient is autistic, get their specific wants and needs from them as best as you can. It could make a huge difference in their life.
