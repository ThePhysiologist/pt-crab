Issue 016

Pain Maps for Patellar Tendinopathy, Electric Dry Needling for Subacromial Pain Syndrome, Racial Trauma in Rehab Medicine, and High Intensity SNFs

This week, PT Crab brings you heads, shoulders, knees, and SNFs. We’ve got a new, interesting protocol for easy and early detection of patellar tendinopathy that shows promise, a new use for electric dry needling (and an image I’ll never forget from that paper. You’ll have to click through the link to see it), a meta piece about the persistent racial trauma in rehab medicine and how we can help patients who deal with it, and high-intensity training in a SNF. It worked and no one died! Yay!

Let’s dive in.

A Single Leg Squat Can (Possibly) Identify Patellar Tendinopathy

The Gist -This research, from a collection of PhDs from Queensland, Australia, presents a new method for early detection of patellar tendinopathy, pain maps. The researchers gathered 57 teenage volleyball players and had them perform single-leg decline squats, then indicate if they had pain and, if so, where. The where was identified using a collection of pain maps that let the researchers easily identify and describe the pain location. 12% had patellar tendon pain with this motion and, upon further research using ultrasound, were seen to have thicker patellar tendons, greater disability, and more frequent neovascularization than the other groups. All of these are evidence of patellar tendinopathy. It seems that a single leg squat that shows patellar tendon pain may be enough to warn about tendinopathy.

Tell Me More -The researchers found that 15 of their participants had some type of knee pain. Of these, 5 who had “other knee pain”, a moniker that they used for non-patellar pain. Neither they nor the pain-free participants had remarkable patellar tendons, unlike the patellar tendon pain participants. Since patellar tendinopathy has a high prevalence in adult volleyball players (45%) and is the most frequent overuse injury in adolescent, elite athletes (according to the paper), being able to quickly and easily identify it could be effective in early treatment. That’s what this test promises though it is still early in development.

The study is limited by its small sample size, limited population tested, and limited clinical exams they performed on the participants. The researchers acknowledge this, but indicate that it’s still promising though we’re not yet sure how promising it is. A source they cite in the paper indicates that clinical examination reports lower prevalence rates of tendinopathy than ultrasonography does, so if this simple squat test could bring those values together, that could be beneficial for easily identifying tendinopathy. We’re still a ways away from this becoming a real test with sensitivity and specificity numbers, but it may be on its way there and is already clinically useful to get a better understanding of the nature of a patient’s pain.

Zap Your SAP Away. Electric Dry Needling for Subacromial Pain Syndrome

The Gist -This randomized trial compared spinal thrust manipulation to thrust manipulation plus electric dry needling for subacromial pain syndrome. Manipulation plus needling was better than just manipulation. Yay! Though they both worked. Double yay! A 6 week treatment period followed by 2 week, 4 week, and 3 month follow up saw needled patients reduce pain by .7 more at 2 weeks, 1.2 more at 4, and 2.1 at three months. They also reduced SPADI-assessed disability by 17.9 at three months. Both of these are above MCIDs. Triple yay! Why did electric dry needling work better? Read on MacDuff.

Tell Me More -The researchers chose electric dry needling due to superior analgesia associated with electric v regular dry needling and how it may block inflammatory cytokines and other inflammatory factors relative to regular dry needling. What manipulations did they do and where did they stab? Thanks for asking.

They performed manipulations to the cervicothoracic spine (C4 - T9) and rib articulations (1st - 3rd) in alignment with previous research that showed how this helps with subacromial pain syndrome. The needling was aimed at 8 trigger points in the anterolateral and posterolateral sulbcromial, lateral brachial, and scapular regions in all patients, while 6 other points were at the discretion of the treating therapist, who found particularly stiff or tender spots via palpatory examination. There were some side effects from the electric dry needling that included bruising in 15 patients and nausea in 2. There were no reported side effects for the thrust-only group.

This study breaks some new ground in the treatment of subacromial pain syndrome and is a well-written, easy to read example of a great research article.

Racial Trauma in Rehab Medicine

The Gist -During a summer that introduced new thoughts, ideas, and realizations to many of us, the Archives of PM&R joined right in, publishing this editorial piece on how to reckon with racial trauma in rehabilitation medicine. I am in the fortunate place not to have had to consider this before, as the piece makes clear that most of its readers will be. It’s an article that’s tough to summarize, short, and an easy read, so I highly recommend that you go check it out yourself. Do please read it.

Tell Me More -If I must summarize, here are the authors’ main points:
- Systemic trauma and health are linked in a way that we don’t fully understand.
- Not studying this link will allow the health issues we treat to continue into future generations.
- The trauma-informed care model should be a starting point when treating patients.

  • In the case of a patient facing amputation, for example, a broad trauma-informed approach would rigorously inquire about the structural circumstances such as racism, economic inequality, and access disparities leading to limb loss as well as downstream barriers that might impair prosthesis acquisition and rehabilitation outcomes.
  • The makeup of care teams should better reflect those for whom they care
  • State-sanctioned violence, sociopolitical disenfranchisement, racial prejudice, and economic injustice are grinding down on too many of our patients. For their sake and for the health of our country, these social determinants of health demand a response from our profession.

Where is this editorial of which you speak? Here. Please read it. It is open access and just two pages long.

High-Intensity Training, Coming to a SNF Near You

The Gist -A group of researchers from Colorado did a quite interesting implementation study on high-intensity functional resistance training in a SNF and came up with some great results. They split participants non-randomly into an i-Stronger protocol group (i thought we were done with “i”’s, but i guess not…) and a regular exercise group and let them at it. The i-Stronger group received a higher intensity functional rehab program that was strictly planned and advanced to push patients to their maximal capabilities, intentionally keeping weight at the 8-rep threshold (80% of 1-RM) and functional exercises at just short of failure, advancing patients rapidly to keep them there. PTs were trained on the protocol and documented its implementation.

The i-Stronger patients were more satisfied with their PT, refused sessions less often, had no more adverse events, and increased their gait speeds and Short Physical Performance Battery tests in clinically important ways. It was a win for the more intense exercise and the designers are looking to roll it out more broadly soon.

Tell Me More -The non-random above probably stuck out to you, eh? It stuck out to me too. They had quite strict inclusion criteria due to the intense nature of the exercise. That may skew the gait speed and SPPB improvements a bit. The patient satisfaction portion is interesting, but it’s important to keep in mind that the difference wasn’t huge (6%) and that this study was done in a veteran facility which may skew the group to preferring more intense exercise. The lack of adverse events is great news though, giving the study a clear outcome: high-intensity rehabilitation in a SNF is safe and feasible. I’ll be tracking this protocol in the literature and see what else these researchers have on it in the next few years.
