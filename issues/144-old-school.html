What to do with Osteoporosis?

The Gist -Honestly, the results of this one were really surprising. Like I said above, this is a clinical practice guideline release by the Journal of Geriatric Physical Therapy titled: PT Management of Patients with Suspected or Confirmed Osteoporosis. What stood out immediately is that they don’t make any recommendations for exercise to reverse bone mineral density loss because there isn’t evidence that it can. The best we can do is slow the loss of bone density. But we can indeed do that and we can do it pretty safely and simply.

The CPG points to good evidence that static weight bearing exercise like standing on one foot can slow bone density loss in the hip in postmenopausal people. They also recommend long-duration “adequately dosed” progressive resistance training in non-weight-bearing as a method to slow bone density loss in both the hip and lumbar spine. This means high load open or closed kinetic chain stuff like squats, overhead presses, deadlifts, and the like. But it’s gotta be hard to be useful. Meanwhile, high force weight-bearing exercises weren’t so hot in their analysis. This includes things like running, jumping, and dancing. The researchers who stitched this together postulate that the lack of efficacy of these dynamic activities could be due to “stress reactions or structural failure due to excessive overload in women with osteoporosis.”

It’s important to remember that all of the above is in postmenopausal folks, a higher risk group than the premenopausal that we’ll discuss below. Combined exercise showed efficacy in both groups. That’s a combination of low force closed kinetic chain stuff (balance training, endurance work, squats) with high force open kinetic chain activities (overhead presses, gym equipment, etc.). A combination of jogging and stair climbing, for example, was effective, as was a combo of aerobics with high-intensity OKC exercise.

What about pre-menopausal people? Read on.

Tell Me More -In people who haven’t been through menopause yet, the recommendations are a bit different. The researchers don’t recommend static weight-bearing exercise and don’t have adequate evidence to say anything about dynamic low force weight bearing exercise (walking, Tai Chi, etc.). High force dynamic exercise, like jogging, stair climbing, and CKC weight training, are recommended and combined exercise including high force OKC exercise is highly recommended. This is the stuff we would expect.

For men, there was insufficient evidence to make any recommendations.

Even with what we know from above, there’s a problem here: all of these interventions take a long time—like 6 months to two years long—to make any difference. This is something the researchers are sensitive to, since many people covered in these recommendations will be on Medicare. They posit an idea to get it done though

For patients with access to Medicare, physical therapists’ services are a covered benefit, with no limitations on care (related to time, visits, or cost) if the services provided are medically necessary and skilled. This includes maintenance care and care to prevent a decline in function. A new model for physical therapy management of patients with osteoporosis could involve concluding and then resuming multiple short episodes of care over an extended time period.
You’d have to have a relationship with a physician to ensure they’re signing off on plans of care if you wanted to make this work, but you could indeed make it work, depending on your setting.

The other thing they note is that being able to exercise regularly is actually pretty complicated and that we as PTs should look into our patients’ social determinants of health when designing a plan they need to stick to for long periods. I for example, work with many low income clients and thus provide programs that can be done completely inside the home with household objects and often make referrals to Silver Sneakers programs as well. If you have good tactics to help lower income seniors work out more often, feel free to leave the details in a comment here.

Can we fix kyphosis?

The Gist -No.

But we can prevent it from advancing as rapidly and possibly make it decline a little bit. This long-term-focused piece from the Journal of Geriatric PT wanted to know if a short-term kyphosis program could have long term results and it looks like the answer was yes. They initially enrolled 103 people with a mean age of 70 and kyphosis of 52 degrees (normal is 20-40 degrees and a problem is usually greater than 45). They completed a 2x/wk, 3 month long group exercise and postural training program that focused on spinal mobility, spinal extensor strength, and postural alignment that advanced intensity to keep it challenging for folks (7-8/10 on RPE). People were also educated on what good spinal posture looked like and encouraged to check on their posture three times per day.

This exercise was effective in the short term for reducing kyphosis, increasing lumbar lordosis, increasing gait speed, and increasing ability in the Time Loaded Standing test. But we’re thinking long term, cuz what’s the point if it doesn’t keep working? Well good news, it keeps working.

At this 3 year followup, 43 people from the original study came back and continued to demonstrate reduced kyphosis or kyphotic development relative to the control group from the original RCT. Their ability on the Time Loaded Standing test continued to beat the control group, and their maximal gait speed and lordosis were also significantly increased. At the end of the study, the some measures were increased even more than they were after the initial three month period. The Physical Activity for the Elderly scale, the Time Loaded Standing test, the lordosis measurement, and the max gait speed. Kyphosis wasn’t improved as much at three years as it was at 3 months but it was still better relative to control.

Tell Me More -All of those effects above are relative to control groups at the two time points, three months and three years. They’re not relative to the individuals themselves prior to beginning the program. That’s important, since we wouldn’t expect gait speed to be significantly improved over the course of a three year period as our group ages from 70 to 73, but it didn’t decay as much as the control group’s gait speed.

For a direct pre to post measurement person by person, kyphosis decreased 1.5 degrees at three years relative to pre-treatment while it initially decreased 3.8 degrees after the three months of exercise.

Basically, everything got not as bad and some things even got better three years later. In an aged cohort, this is fantastic news. I know you may have some concerns that those who returned for 3 year followup were significantly different than the overall sample, and they were, but only in the Timed Loaded Standing test, no others, meaning they were a pretty good representation of the whole group while not being perfect.

What works best for people at risk of fracture?

The Gist -Carrying on from the CPG above, let’s talk a little bit about how to train people who are at risk of fracture. This means people with osteoporosis, but not always. This systematic review and meta analysis that I’ll briefly cover wanted to know if progressive resistance training was effective They included 53 studies on men and women over 50 with low bone density or a history of fractures. Overall, the analysis showed that progressive resistance training decreased pain, improved performance on the TUG, increased femoral neck density (but not lumbar spine), and reduced pain. There wasn’t evidence to suggest that it reduced the total number of falls or fall risk of people. It wasn’t a focus of the paper, but the researchers point out in the Discussion section that this type of training is associated with a lower risk of death from all causes when performed alone or alongside aerobic training. A 23% reduction in all-cause mortality and 31% in cancer-related mortality.

An important part of all of this was the “progressive” part of progressive resistance training. Adequate loading was seen across many of these studies and the researchers in this review emphasize the importance of providing adequate challenge in order to see adaptation. In general, old folks are more capable than you’d think! If you let them try.

Tell Me More -There’s not a ton more to say, just that you need to load your geriatric patients to see these results, so give them some weights!
