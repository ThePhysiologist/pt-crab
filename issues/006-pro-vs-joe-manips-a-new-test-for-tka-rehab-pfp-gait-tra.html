Issue 006

Pro vs Joe Manips, A New Test for TKA Rehab, PFP Gait Training, and A New Approach to Goal Setting in LBP

This week’s edition is another broad range, as you’ve come to expect. We have variances across the body and also in the seriousness of the studies reviewed. We start light-hearted with an interesting one comparing the kinematics of professional manipulators vs. newbies and students, continue to a new sitting-rising test for TKA recovery, amp up the complexity with a piece about gait training to fix PFP, and finish with the biggest, most complex one about patient-led goal setting and how it could revolutionize chronic LBP treatment.

Let’s dive in!

Do you manip like a pro?

The Gist -This relatively light-hearted study from the journal of Musculoskeletal Science and Practice compares the kinetics and kinematics of experienced manipulators to that of PT students and residents to find the differences. They recruited 4 experts, 11 residents, 13 third-years, and 15 first-years to perform 4 reps of a lumbar manipulation (a twist manip in side-lying, AKA the Sidelying Lumbar Rotation with Locking Manipulation according to the FAAOMPT) and captured a bunch of data about how they performed it. The differences between the different experience levels were huge and quite interesting. Details in Tell Me More below.

The major takeaway: experts applied much more of the force with their lower halves, using their bodies and shifting their weight to perform the manipulations. The less experienced used their hands more, not their legs. The pros also had almost double the peak velocity in their manips, and less of a “wind-up” and “bounce-back” to the motion. So if you want to be pro: use your legs, be quick and smooth, don’t wind up first.

Tell Me More -As practitioners gained more experience (first-years —> third-years —> residents) their techniques more closely imitated the experienced practitioners, but were still a ways off. Experts demonstrated a greater displacement of their vertical and horizontal masses. They shifted weight smoothly from the cephalad foot to the caudad foot as their COM shifted up and then back down into the motion.

Experts also rotated their pelvises the opposite direction of the more amateur practitioners. The amateurs twisted away from the patients, while the experts twisted into them, using their pelvis to push the patients’ in a superior-anterior direction. This resulted in greater patient pelvic rotation than the other groups as well. Overall, they were smoother, faster, and had a slightly different technique than the less experienced. This isn’t surprising, as it demonstrates the economy of movement that comes with the practice of a skill. The study provides a potential shortcut to expert-level manipulation by detailing the modifications that could improve your ability. It’s pretty short and worth a glance if you want the full details. Don’t get bogged down in the tables, just focus on the discussion and charts.

Get More TKA Data with the Floor Sitting-Rising Test

The Gist -There are a lot of ways to test progress in recovery from TKA, but this newish one from the Archives of PM&R seems to get at something that others don’t. They enrolled 240 participants with unilateral primary TKA and had them perform the sitting-rising test at baseline and after 12 weeks of an exercise program.

To perform the SRT, patients are told “Without worrying about the speed of movement, try to sit and then rise from the floor, using the minimum of support that you believe is needed.” After a trial run, the test is scored out of 10 — 5 for the down phase and 5 for the up. Patients start with full marks and lose one point each time they use their hands to push themselves up or legs to kneel. It’s easier to watch, so here’s a 2 minute video of Dan “Golden Smile, Goldener Guns” Reynan demonstrating it over on YouTube.

The researchers found that SRT scores converged with all performance-based tests they used alongside it (TUG, 6-min walk, 30s sit-to-stand, stair climb, 4-meter walk, single leg balance, and 40 meter fast walk) and that it was comparably responsive. It’s a simple, quick activity to add more data about functional ability that tracked with other common tests at three and six months.

Tell Me More -Performers at baseline had stronger hip abductors and knee extensors along with more knee flexion and extension ROM than non-performers. They had similar self-efficacy scores to non-performers, but less fear of falling. Getting nerdy (hey, that’s what this section is for, you asked for it), the 3 and 6 month effect sizes of the SRT were small to moderate (0.38 and 0.42) meaning that changed scores moved enough to be notable, but not a ton. The Guyatt index was small (.25 and .19 at 3 and 6 months), meaning that the test does discriminate between improved and unimproved individuals (using the Global Rating of Change scale to determine improved vs. unimproved), but not massively.

Overall, the test’s measurements aren’t earth-shattering, but do add to a clinician’s arsenal when testing the recovery level of patients with TKA. SRT is a complex, challenging performance based test that gets at information that other tests don’t. It’s also a great measure of all-cause mortality in next 6 years the general population, and the BBC made a video about that too.

These Knees are Made for Walking - Functional Gait Training for PFP

The Gist -PFP is an oft-covered Crab topic, as it’s an oft-researched one as well. The 2016 clinical guidelines out of the Patellofemoral Pain Research Retreat (who wouldn’t love to be invited to that?) recommend conservative approaches via exercise therapy and foot orthoses to correct it. These researchers said “nah, bro, Imma go my own way.”

Instead of exercise, they used fancy equipment in their 3D Motion Analysis lab to identify gait deviations in their 31 participants and correct them on an individualized level. They took three measurements - two pre-treatment and one post, then analyzed for individual problems and corrected them through functional retraining. They had 3 weeks of walking correction then 3 of squatting, following common progression principles. At 8 weeks, 18 of the 31 were pain free and that continued through 6 months. 10 reported mild pain and only 3 reported moderate, down from 12 and 15 before the intervention. Maybe consider gait training for PFP then? It looks good from here.

Tell Me More -Gotcha. We’ll dive into the outcome measures a bit more here to dig into what really changed. Their primary outcome measure was the gait deviation recovery, and secondary were the anterior knee pain scale for function, the normal, numeric scale for pain taken at weeks 1 and 8 then months 3 and 6, and the Likert (long-term recovery) scale at 6 months. The researchers don’t individualize the changes, so we can see the magnitude of them but overall pain values dropped across the board. Pre-intervention, 74% of participants (23 of them) were at severe or moderate functional impairment and just 26% (8) were at minor. Post intervention, 77% (24) reached minor impairment and just 20% were left at moderate, with 0% severe and 3% none (1 person). At three and six months, the signs were even better, as more of the minor impaired slid into the no impairment level. Pain followed a similar course.

What about the deviations they worked on? 5 people had decreased knee flexion in stance, 10 had increased bilateral knee extension throughout the gait, 8 had increased peak hip adduction and 8 more had decrease left peak hip IR. Surprisingly, 11 of the 31 did not demonstrate clinically significant changes in these factors, but pain and function still improved across the board.

Improve LBP Results By Changing What You Say

The Gist -You know those click-bait articles we always see at the end of news stories about the “One Weird Trick To (Blank)”? Well this next study seems like one of those, but appears to really work. Last year in the British Journal of Sports Medicine, researchers published a piece of how patient-led goal setting reduced chronic LBP pain and disability at 12 months. It’s a very interesting RCT.

They randomly assigned 75 patients to intervention or control, and used back pain disability and intensity as their main outcome measures, with a lot of secondary ones. They were assessed immediately after treatment (2mo), 4mo, and then 12mo. What was the treatment? Control got a standard exercise program that included a bit of time talking to their therapist at baseline, 2mo, and 4mo. Treatment got a lot more. They were put through patient-led goal-setting, where the therapist took them through 5 face-to-face sessions at 2 week intervals, they also got an educational handbook about neuroscience, goal-setting, and tips for self-management. The patient set goals and strategies to work on between sessions and used a workbook to track their progress and barriers. No explicit instructions were given by the PT.

Here’s what happened (a bit truncated, cuz there’s a lot): at two and four months, pain was down 3.3 points in the intervention and 1 in the control, disability (according to the Quebec Pain Disability Scale) was down 21 in the intervention and 7 in the control, SF-36 quality of life scale was up 16 in the intervention and \<1 in the control, and there’s much more that we can’t fit. Overall, patient-led goal-setting, even without exercise, made a big difference in these patients’ lives.

Tell Me More -I think the better statement here may be: does it matter? And it’s a good question. Neither of these was anywhere near a standard PT approach to chronic LBP. The intervention also requires significant experience with patient-led goal setting to pull off. It’s not the most clinically applicable study we’ve ever seen, but it does open a door to a new technique by backing up its theoretical value. The study shows that letting a patient lead the goal setting process and educating them well about it can lead to decreased pain and depression and increased self-efficacy and quality of life, even without physical therapy intervention. Since we know that manual PT and exercise help on their own, combining them with patient-led goal setting could be a real step forward in the management of this condition. But enough editorializing. Details:

The study has some limitations and a lot more data that can be accessed in the paper, but I’ll just highlight a few. The authors concede that the “difference in therapeutic consultation time may have affected the outcomes” as the goal-setting group spent a lot more time with their PT than the other. They also don’t have any data on patient adherence to either system. There is good data that this approach works for other conditions, but this is also the first study of its kind in PT, so we don’t have a complex understanding of it yet.

Lastly, what is patient-led goal setting anyway? I’ll let the authors explain:

(1) education about the neuroscience and psychology of pain; (2) patient-led goal setting, where the patient identifies issues or problems that matter most to them, sets the goals and develops strategies to achieve these goals with guidance from the healthcare professional; and (3) individual feedback with collaborative discussion on barriers and progression of the strategies. This approach is supported by the healthcare professional; however, the patient drives the process and is encouraged to undertake the steps towards goal achievement.
