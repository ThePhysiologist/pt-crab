And now, let’s dive in!

Let’s make NMES better, shall we?

The Gist -You use NMES, I use NMES, we all wumbo. And if you get that reference, then you’ll also know that smelly smell that smells smelly. But anyway, this one came up because I’m working with someone who had a patella repair recently (it broke into three pieces, ouch!) and she’s got some serious quad lag. So yea, we’re looking at using NMES. But I figured I’d try to do it well, so I looked into this 2021 paper from Exercise Sports Science Reviews. A global group of researchers got together to talk about ways to enhance adaptations to NMES and came up with some ideas. We’ll start simple and increase in complexity as we go.

First off, pulse durations and frequency. The common approach is short, biphasic pulses at 30-50Hz, according to the paper. But what if you change it up? They recommend trying out wider pulses at higher frequencies, while remembering that this can lead to earlier fatigue, it could lead to better muscle engagement too. They’re talking pulse durations of 0.5 - 1.0ms and frequencies of 80-100Hz.

The other simple approach they discuss is not placing electrodes on muscle bellies, but rather on nerve trunks. This is only feasible in a few areas, but could provide better, more complete stimulation of the muscle. In fact, this is how the first NMES was applied to increase dorsiflexion in folks with foot drop. Pursuant to that, the paper discusses a couple of areas where you could put leads: femoral nerve trunk, fibular nerve, and tibial nerve. So give that a shot if you want.

Tell Me More -There are some other ideas that are more complex to set up too. One of them is distributed NMES, where you use multiple electrodes spread all over the muscle and on synergists as well, then the currents are rotated through electrodes. This would be tough to set up in clinic, since you’d probably need two machines running at the same time, but is has been shown to produce contractions that reduce fatigue by 25%, so it may be worth your time.

Lastly, the researchers discuss adding BFR to NMES, attempting to increase the neuromuscular fatigue. I’m going to assume you’re already familiar with BFR, so let’s just talk about combining it with NMES. BDF has been shown to assist in the recruitment of type II muscle fibers at lower intensities, helping upregulate hypertrophic pathways. The researchers cite a study that showed that recreationally active people receiving a combo of BFR and NMES gained more muscle than those using either BFR or NMES alone, but this hasn’t been tested on injured folks.

There are a couple more approaches in the paper that I didn’t add here, so if you’re interested, give it a look. If not, don’t. I don’t care.

What PT does for Sexual Health

The Gist -If you’v been reading the Crab for a while, you know I’m really into sex and sexual health. If you haven’t been reading the Crab for a while, you know that now. That’s why I couldn’t scroll past this piece from Sexual Medicine Reviews (an excellent journal, btw) that looks into what physical therapy can do for sexual and pelvic dysfunction. If you’re already into pelvic health, this piece probably isn’t for you, since it’s quite general. But if you’re pelvic health curious, whether you’re looking to know more so you can refer more effectively or you’re looking at getting into the practice yourself, this overview is excellent.

The opening line is on point and often forgotten, “Sexual dysfunction affects not only intimacy, but an individual’s sense of identity and self-worth.” It’s also underreported and inadequately treated. I think every PT should be asking about sex and sexual health to all their patients, especially ones coming in for hip, jaw, and back problems, because sex is a really important part of being human and having close relationships, and we don’t talk about it enough. But I digress.

Clinically speaking, the area of the paper that’s probably best reading is the anatomy section. It’s a quick and clear rundown of what’s going on down there, one layer at a time. The only way I’m going to dig into it now (for the sake of brevity) is to point out the innervation of the pelvic area. That’s up to the pudendal, ilioinguinal, iliohypogastric, genitofemoral, and posterior femoral cutaneous nerves. That includes nerve roots L1-L4 and S3-S5 as well, so it’s a lot of lumbar stuff. The obturator internus (a sneaky little external rotator) also shares an attachment with the levator ani and has fascia that’s pierced by the pudendal nerve at Alcock’s canal (aptly named, I’d say). So all this stuff can be important to pelvic function.

Tell Me More -Moving beyond anatomy, there are basically (very basically) two types of pelvic floor dysfunction, overactivity and underactivity. For a very brief summary:

Men with overactive pelvic floor muscles and myofascial trigger points may report pain in the testicles, groin, tip of the penis, and abdomen. They also may report inability to achieve an erection, premature ejaculation, difficulty reaching climax, or postejaculatory pain. Women may report symptoms of vulvar burning or itching, clitoral pain, and vaginal pain. In addition, men and women experiencing pelvic floor dysfunction may report bowel and bladder symptoms not related to sexual function. These symptoms can include constipation, particularly outlet dysfunction and dyssynergia, voiding dysfunction, urinary urgency and frequency, as well as urinary and fecal incontinence.
On the underactive side:
Laxity of connective tissue and pelvic floor muscle weakness can diminish support of pelvic viscera contributing to pelvic organ prolapse (POP). Typically, symptoms related to POP worsen over the course of the day…Incontinence of urine, stool, or gas can occur secondary to pelvic floor muscle underactivity. This incontinence can affect men, women, and children. Stress urinary incontinence, in particular, is a result of increased abdominal pressure from a cough, laugh, sneeze, or lift and can be associated with underactive pelvic floor and abdominal wall muscles, respiratory diaphragm impairment, POP, or prostatectomy.

The hips are an important area, as you may expect. Hips with limited motion or stability can lead to the body seeking that stability and motion from the pelvic floor, meaning you can have increased pelvic obliquity or lack of pelvic floor relaxation, leading to pain with penetrative sex (for either the giving partner or the receiver) or post-orgasmic or ejaculatory pain.

This paper has a LOT more, including discussions of assessment, general overviews of treatment, and a bit about patient eduction as well. It’s really great and ideal for students or those with limited experience in this are to learn a lot about it, fast. Hard recommend.

You may not know about ITBS

The Gist -Everything you know about treating ITBS in runners could be wrong. Or it could be right. The truth is, we don’t really know, at least according to this Austro-Spanish (I wonder if that empire would’ve been broken up as easily as the Hungarians…) team publishing in Physical Therapy in Sport. They dive into a mathematical model to discuss pathogenic factors of IT band syndrome then take a second dive (a real Tom Daley move, without the knitting) to see what we know about treating it. Their mathy model explores the physical expectations of IT band pain and predict some remedies that their dive into the literature doesn’t bear out. Overall, a really interesting piece.

But what did they actually say? Well. First off, according to a study they cite from 2013, only 44% of people recover from ITBS during 8 weeks of therapy. So this is an important area. The semi-systematic review they did reveals that soft tissue techniques are theoretically plausible but the research on them is limited so far. Hip strengthening is also plausible and has been demonstrated to be effective, but little pure strength research has been done. And lastly, stretching of the glute med, TFL, and glute max has shown some transient effects and are theoretically plausible, but we don’t know. So… yea.

What we do know is summed up in their practical recommendations studded throughout:
- Soft tissue techniques should focus on hypertonic muscles adjacent to ITB
- Soft tissue techniques are effective in reducing symptoms and can, therefore, be applied in acute phases
- Hip muscle exercises should focus on neuromuscular control and strength endurance rather than maximum strength
- To prevent undesired side effects, strength training should be followed by measures to reduce muscle tone
- Static ITB stretches should be performed for a total of at least 3 min (e.g. 3 ( 1 min stretching) to reduce muscle tone, particularly after hip and thigh muscle strengthening measures
- Lasting effects may only be expected when stretching exercises are incorporated into training routines and repeated on a regular basis

So that’s actually quite useful, I’d say.

Tell Me More -Lastly, they looked into running retraining. This is gait retraining separate from muscle strengthening, kapeesh? But there’s very little data to back it up so far. So we don’t really know. But again, we have some practical recommendations:
- Running retraining should be based on the individual assessment of running mechanics
- Real-time feedback should mostly be given in the early phases of rehabilitation to correct aberrant movement patterns, but then be gradually reduced through the course of treatment

So what have we learned today? It’s that we don’t know as much as we thought. We do know that a lot of our work on ITBS is based on tradition and expert opinion and that we need to do more work. We also know that these authors have a significant bias toward a pathoanatomical process of pain and give very limited shrift to a more complex biopsychosocial one. I know that ITBS isn’t chronic low back pain, but that doesn’t mean there’s not room for a BPS approach.

What this paper does well is process through the clinical reasoning behind ITBS and posit possible solutions while backing them up with the relatively limited amount of evidence we do have. It’s a good read, all things considered.

And if you’re interested in an even deeper dive into ITBS, including our (mostly) complete history of how we’ve treated it, see Paul Geisler’s work from PT Crab 61.
