(All of) The Biomechanics of a Squat

The Gist -This excellent review from Rachel Straub and Christopher Powers at USC breaks down the biomechanics of squatting joint by joint. It is top-notch. I obviously don’t have time to go through everything here, so we’re going to talk highlights. But if you like squatting, or if you want to better rehab hip, knee, and lumbar injuries, this is an incredible resource. I’m going to cut the details a little bit to make it more actionable, but the details are well worth a look too. Without further ado, let’s go through it.

Trunk positioning - a more forward trunk shifts the line of gravity anteriorly, decreasing the knee flexion moment and increasing the hip flexion one. Lumbar spine flexion angles being equal, this means that a more anterior trunk increases the amount of work hip and lumbar extensors have to do and decreases the amount of work that knee extensors have to do.

Tibial inclination - The more upright your tibia is, the less of a knee flexion moment exists, so the less you have to use your quads. Heel elevation creates the opposite situation, it allows for a more forward tibia with less dorsiflexion, increasing the knee flexion moment and thus, quad activation.

Foot rotation - changing foot rotation can modify sagittal and transverse plane moments. “For example, rotating the feet outward 30° has been reported to decrease the valgus moment at the knee by 50% while simultaneously increasing the varus moment by 80%,” but it has no effect on the activation of quads, hamstrings, or gastroc.

That’s all rotations, what about width and depth? I’ll quick.

Width - It won’t be surprising that a wide stance creates more of a knee valgus and hip ER moment. The knee and hip flexion moment data is mixed, we don’t know one way or another. It does seem that a wide squat recruits more of glute max and less gastroc, but that’s all we can tell.

Depth - Knee and hip flexion moments both increase as squats get deeper, unsurprisingly. But there are multiple ways to get deeper and stay balanced. You can increase trunk inclination more or tibial inclination. And there’s a cool interplay here that is the most interesting part of this portion of the paper.

Biasing your squat for knee or hip extensors

The relationship between the trunk and the tibia is what determines knee and hip extensor bias of your squat. In brief, when the degree of trunk inclination exceeds tibial inclination by more than 10°, the squat is hip extensor biased. When tibia exceeds trunk by more than 10°, it’s knee extensor biased. If angles are about the same, so is the work of the extensors.

But What About in Clinic?

Yea, let’s talk about how to apply this to patients with knee and hip problems. The paper gives an excellent breakdown. Here we go.

Patellofemoral Pain - You need to strengthen hips and knees for this, but it’s important not to flare up the pain too much. Early on, bias squats toward the hips to reduce patellofemoral compression. That’s upright tibia, forward trunk. You can bias more for glute max by adding a band to the knees or widening the squat too. Progress the activity toward more and more quads activation throughout rehab.

ACL Reconstruction - Bias squats toward the hip to reduce the anterior knee pain that usually comes with ACLR. But keep that stance more narrow to avoid excessive valgus. Deeper squats and narrower squats with mild toe out are good to advance to as rehab progresses.

FAI - Limit squat depth to available hip flexion ROM. You can see when hip flexion ROM runs out because the pelvis will tilt posteriorly at the end of hip flexion. Keep squats more shallow, even if patients don’t have pain, since deep squats stress out the hip.

LBP - Minimize compression and shear loads on the lumbar spine and activate hip muscles. That means you need to use a wider stance to allow for more hip activation with less forward trunk lean. Wide stance brings less ankle dorsiflexion, making “a favorable trunk-tibia relationship to produce a hip bias.” Limit posterior pelvic tilt here too to keep the spine neutral.

Tibiofemoral OA - Lessen knee compression at first, then slowly progress into more and more, just like with PFP. Keep squats shallow to limit knee compression, since tibiofemoral contact area shrinks with increasing squat depth, meaning pressure is greater since it’s a function of area. Watch out for wide stances, which increase tibiofemoral compression. Also limit outward foot rotation for medial OA (the common one) and use it for lateral OA.

What if I want to know more about squatting?

The Gist -Then you’re SOL. But I can tell you about lifting. Specifically, stoop lifting versus squat versus freestyle. Researchers got folks to lift a 15kg (33lb) box either however they wanted, with a squat technique, or with a stoop, no legs. These were all healthy, young folks, because the idea was to see what shear and compressive loads looked like at different levels of the lumbar spine. Here’s what the lifts looked like.

The results are going to surprise you.

Stoop lifting created the lowest compressive and total loads throughout the spine. Squat and freestyle were about the same throughout the spine until you got to L3/L4 and L5/S1, where freestyle created significantly greater loads. But there’s more than one type of load, that was just compressive.

Shear loads were greatest in stoop lifting at every level except L5/S1. Here’s a breakdown of what those different loads mean in context of the spine.

Shear loads in stoop lifting at L4/L5 were 180% of those experienced at that level with squat lifting while they were 800% higher with stoop lifting at the T12/L1 level. It makes a big difference.

Tell Me More - That was a lot, but sure, I got more. Did you know that 90% of all spondylolisthesis and herniated discs occur at L5/S1? So it’s important to keep shear down here. That would mean stoop and squat are best in this area, but there aren’t massive differences. And different biomechanical/mathematical models demonstrate slightly different forces, so take all of this with a grain of salt.

Loads for all lifting styles increased caudally, but differences between loads increased rostrally. The closer you get to the pelvis, the less affect these changes have. Overall, freestyle lifting seems the worst for the combination of compressive and shear loads involved, while squat and stoop are close depending on what level you want to limit. The paper has some excellent graphs of the load at each level that are easy to analyze, so check it out to see the details.
