Issue 172

Meniscus Repair, Protocol, and PT

This week we’re going into the knee. Deep into the knee. With a discussion of whether people should have a meniscectomy at all, what to do if they do have a meniscal surgery, and a quick refresher on the CPGs. It’s to the point, so let’s dive in.

People Shouldn’t Get a Meniscectomy

The Gist -I’m sure it’s good for some people, but quite a few studies have shown that conservative treatment (that’s us) is better than meniscectomy in a few populations. That includes degenerative tears and traumatic tears in the younger and the middle aged. This specific paper is about PT vs. surgery in degenerative medial meniscus posterior root tears and was from BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders in 2021. In an 8 year period, 90 patients got a meniscectomy and 56 were treated with PT alone. According to their pain rating, the Tegner activity scale, the Lysholm knee scoring scale, and the progress of OA at a follow up of 6.3 years on average, everybody got better with no significant differences. Except one.

The progression of knee OA was significantly greater in people who had meniscectomies compared to people who didn’t. This was determined via X-ray that looked at the hip-knee-ankle angle that shows us how the medial joint space is doing. And it wasn’t doing well after the meniscectomy.

Tell Me More -Everyone did improve whether they got surgery or just PT, and we don’t have information on what type of PT these folks had. But long-term, it looks like PT is better. Meniscectomy provided more rapid relief of pain, but wasn’t as good later on. More surgeons have been trying to preserve menisci in the last few years, and that’s great. They need to keep doing it.

In your own practice, this article helps with what advice to provide to your patients if they come asking. Many will get PT first before attempting surgery and it’s nice to have longitudinal data like this to see what’s what. You can let them know the long-term outcomes and make better informed decisions.

Return after a Meniscus Repair

The Gist -If you’re looking for a fantastic breakdown of the process to get active again after meniscus repair, this is the place. This review article from Sports Medicine and Arthroscopy breaks things down brilliantly with discussions of each phase of the rehab process. Some, you’ll know by heart. Like the importance of doing a full SLR without quad lag before getting rid of the brace. Or the importance of hip and gluteal strengthening. It’s the return to sport tests that push this review forward.

We’ll skip to those, then loop back below. Their recommended return to sport test battery includes multiple levels:
- 0 out of 10 pain with all activity
- Single Assessment Numerical Evaluation (SANE) rating >90%, Knee injury and OA Score (KOOS) >90, or International Knee Documentation Committee Subjective Knee Form (IKDC) >90
- No active effusion
- Quadriceps girth within 1.5cm bilaterally
- Return to sport testing
- ROM equal or within 2° of contralateral leg
- Isokinetic testing with >90% quad and hamstring similarity and >66% hamstring to quad ratio
- Y balance testing
- Anterior reach within 4cm
- Composite score >90%
- Lateral step-down without dynamic valgus
- Hop testing
- Single and triple hop >90% of contralateral limb
It’s a very good, complete list, adapted from ACL guidelines.

Tell Me More -Most post-op protocols aren’t based on good evidence. The ones in this paper are developed based on evidence and well designed. They separate between simple and complex tears, break down into phases with clear goals to achieve between them, and include patient-rated concepts like confidence in their knee. It’s a much better protocol than I’ve seen before and I highly recommend checking it out. That’s all in the open access paper here.

Refreshing the CPG

The Gist -There are clinical practice guidelines for a lot of this, last published in 2018. Like any CPG, they’re a bit intense. And there’s not room to discuss all of them here, but I do want to go into the diagnostics portion since we’ve only talked treatment so far. They have a fantastic flowchart of diagnosis for both meniscal tears and articular cartilage lesions in a way that’s much more directly usable than your average CPG.

And, differentially, don’t forget the Ottawa knee rule! It has a sensitivity of .99 and specificity of .49. A knee X-ray is required in patients with any of these criteria:
- Older than 55
- Isolated patellar tenderness
- Tenderness of head of fibula
- Inability to flex knee to 90°
- Inability to bear weight immediately and in ED for 4 steps regardless of limp.

Fun fact! “Clinical examination by well-trained clinicians appears to be as accurate as MRI in regard to the diagnosis of meniscal lesions.” And you can examine better using the flowchart in this CPG.

Tell Me More -I’m going to go into the “Essential Data Elements” here before jumping out of the topic. According to the CPGs, “Clinicians should document the following measures, at least at baseline and discharge or at 1 other follow-up point, for all patients with meniscus tears to support standardization for quality improvement in clinical care and research”. Ready?
- IKDC and KOOS patient reported outcome measures
- 30-second chair stand
- Stair climb test
- TUG
- 6MWT
- Later, Single-leg hop tests
- Modified stroke test for effusion
- Knee AROM
- Isokinetic quads testing
- Forced hyperextension
- Knee PROM
- McMurray’s
- Joint-line tenderness

That’s a lot, yes. And there’s a whole other list for articular cartilage lesions. It’s a great CPG. Big recommend.

What have we learned today?

In this shorter Crab, let’s have a shorter summary too. We learned that meniscectomy is worse long term than PT alone in just about every population. And that there are great resources out there for learning how to treat meniscal repairs and tears better. That includes the protocols put together in the second paper and the examination process in the CPGs. If you wanted to know anything about the meniscus, the sources are right there. Grab ‘em and go!
