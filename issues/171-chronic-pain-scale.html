Do normal pain scales work for chronic pain?

The Gist -They’re not bad. But not great.
This study compared the visual analog scale, the verbal rating scale, the numerical rating scale, and the faces pain scale. You’re probably familiar with the numerical scale (0-10) and the Faces scale, and most have heard of the visual analog scale (though I doubt you use it) where someone points to a point on a line between 0 and 100 to indicate their pain. The verbal rating scale was pretty new to me, but it’s just having someone rate their pain as “none”, “mild”, “moderate”, “severe”, or “very severe”.

The goal of the study was to check out the test-retest reliability of these scales in patients who reported pain unchanged and the sensitivity to positive and negative change. This was all done in Thailand with 250 people who had had pain for at least 3 months.

To be brief, the visual analog scale, numerical rating scale, and faces scale were good at detecting increases. They were also good at being stable.

Tell Me More -So the ones we use are pretty good, it’s true. Though they continue to be subjective measures of pain. The numerical scale we’re all used to, good old Mr. 0-10, was found to be the best at measuring unchanged pain and just about the best at increases and decreases, so it seems to be the way to go.

Measuring chronic pain differently.

The Gist -You’re used to using the NRS in clinic I’d bet. You know, 0-10, etc. And we know that it’s not great. But it’s about the best we have. That’s why these researchers wanted to compare a new scale, the Chronic Pain Behavioral Pain Scale for Adults (CBPS) to the normal method to see if it lines up. They had 101 patients name their pain number and both a researcher and nurse measure pain levels before and after doing some type of “interventional pain procedure” like a steroid injection, trigger point injection, or nerve block to see how the scales aligned.

The CBPS has 5 categories, each scored a 0, 1, or 2. 4 of them are scored by the viewer and 1 by the patient.

From: Pace, A. K., Bruceta, M., Donovan, J., Vaida, S. J., & Eckert, J. M. (2021). An Objective Pain Score for Chronic Pain Clinic Patients. Pain Research & Management, 2021, 6695741. https://doi.org/10.1155/2021/6695741

The idea is that it will get us beyond the “smiling 10/10s” that we see every once in a while and actually figure out how much pain someone is in. For details on how it worked, read on.

Tell Me More -The scale had moderate interrater reliability, which is similar to other observational pain scales like the FLACC or the Critical Care Observational Pain Tool. There was low positive correlation between the NRS and CBPS before the procedure and a moderate one afterward. The NRS showed higher initial pain than the CBPS and things were more similar afterward. Both moved down significantly after the procedure, indicating that they were both able to measure the change.

The downside of this scale at this point is that it’s pretty new and requires some experience. The upside it that it’s relatively objective, at least compared to the NRS. Another concern is that the study was performed with an 88% caucasian, English speaking audience recruited at Penn State Medical Center. Different cultures, groups, countries, and more have different norms about the expression of pain, so that could be a factor here that the trial can’t cover. We need more research (as always). But this is a new way to look at pain and (potentially) a better one.

A better chronic pain scale that already exists.

The Gist -The Graded Chronic Pain Scale exists. You may even have used it before. But what this study presupposes is, it kinda sucks? Or at least is hard to score and doesn’t get enough use because of that. So they made up a new one. Why? Chronic pain is surprisingly hard to measure. Both the number that we’re used to and the effect it has on one’s life. Since high-impact chronic pain is often multi-location, you can track it down and number spots across the whole body with the NRS, but that sucks. And we also know that chronic pain often doesn’t map with any specific physical or anatomical problem. So grading it differently from how we grade “normal” pain could be useful, especially if we’re trying to learn about the pain’s affect on someone’s life rather than simply its intensity.

The revised chronic pain scale asks 6 questions and presents a very simple scoring rubric. They’re both right here:

VonKorff M, DeBar LL, Krebs EE, Kerns RD, Deyo RA, Keefe FJ. Graded chronic pain scale revised: Mild, bothersome and high impact chronic pain. PAIN 2019 Nov 20 doi:10.1097/jpain/0000000000001758

What this scale does is differentiate between no chronic pain, mild chronic pain, bothersome chronic pain, and high impact chronic pain, based on the effect on someone’s life. They have lots of numbers that explain why it’s a good scale that I won’t bore you with right now but the gist of it is that it appears to be a good scale.

Tell Me More -Do you really want all the data? I’ll bet you don’t. And if you do, the paper is available. What I’ll tell you instead is something that you can use. The “differences between people with mild and bothersome chronic pain are predominately characterized by differences in measures of pain coping beliefs, psychological distress, and health perceptions.” These are all things that we can change. Slowly, yes, but we can. I have chronic pain in two areas, my R wrist and my neck. After a few months of OT rehab for my wrist, my OT (shoutout to you, Sejal) just said, “It’s going to hurt. You need to deal with it.” And I did. After a few weeks of thinking she was mean to me, that is. But I got over it. And yea, it hurts, but I can do stuff still, so I deal with it.

That could be the real value in this scale, differentiating people who we can reach via cognitive-behavioral interventions from those who we may not be able to, people with high impact chronic pain.

This is early, yes. But maybe quite useful.

Here’s the new section where I break things down to let you use things quickly. So let’s do that, quickly.

What can you take away from today’s Crab? Love it or hate it, the Numerical Rating Scale for pain is probably the best one to use. For the most part. But if you’re having trouble truly assessing the amount of pain people with chronic pain are feeling, there are options. The best one is probably the revised graded chronic pain scale, especially because it lets you know what kind of an effect the pain is having on someone’s life. Living with 8/10 pain is bad. But if it doesn’t affect your life, it’s not so bad as the person living with 7/10 pain who just can’t get out of bed. So this scale can help discriminate between those cases more effectively. But we don’t yet know how sensitive it is to change yet, since it’s really aimed at chronic cases. It likely changes slowly.
