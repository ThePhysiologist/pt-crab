Issue 020

Tweak Those Scapula Muscles, When Is Your Adductor Fixed, Carpal Stabilizing Taping for Wrist Pain, and Be Conservative to Reduce LBP Fear

This week’s edition features two articles focused on muscles, scapular and hip adductors to be exact. The first is a great study about how to tweak which scapular muscles are working in an exercise by changing it a teeny bit and the second assesses how you can tell when someone’s fully recovered from adductor injuries. It’s a fraught area that these researchers have dug into deeply. After that, we look into carpal stabilizing tape to reduce dorsal wrist pain (and learn more about carpal kinematics) and finish up with a systematic review on how to reduce fear when treating chronic LBP. Be conservative baby.

With that out of the way, let’s dive into the most recent issue.

For More Upper Trap Activation, Bend Your Knee. Wait, What?!

The Gist -This surprising research study from the Journal of Athletic Training shows how small positional tweaks can change scapular muscle activation in really interesting ways. The researchers had their subjects perform bilateral shoulder elevation with external rotation (bent elbows and lifting arms in front of face to above head by flexing shoulders while maintaining tension on theraband between hands) in five different positions and measured EMG activity in Serratus Anterior, Upper Traps, Middle Traps, and Lower Traps to compare. Details of the positions are in the next section, but the gist is this: small tweaks in body position made changes in muscular activity. Perhaps the most surprising is in the title. Doing the same shoulder elevation exercise during dynamic unipedal squat increased Upper Trap activity by 5% and decreased Lower Trap by 5%. Interesting stuff.

Tell Me More -Here are those positions you asked for:
1. Open hands
2. Closed hands
3. Dynamic bipedal squat
4. Static unipedal squat
5. Dynamic unipedal squat

They weren’t in full pistol squat (as I thought at first) rather they flexed their knees to about 30°. Every exercise produced some differences, but the largest were in the lower limb category. It changed the balance between parts of the Traps, emphasizing upper over lower. Closing the hands decreased Middle Trap activation by 3.5% and Lower by almost 8% as well. These little tweaks make pretty big differences to the balance.

What else? Well, they used 31 healthy participants aged about 23 on average, they weren’t the first to show difference like this, and there are some limitations. The biggest is that we don’t yet know what difference this activation makes to the muscles, since they only did it one. Does it change scapular dysfunction rehab? No idea. That needs to be investigated. Should you use it? Maybe. If you want to tweak which part of the Traps get used, this is a super easy way to do it.

Lastly, did you not understand how I explained the exercise? Yea, me either. It’s hard to describe, okay? There are great images and descriptions in the paper.

Since you let me down by poorly describing this, can I read the paper then? Sure, but you don’t have to be so mean about it. It’s here and Open Access because the JAT is awesome.

How Do You Know When Adductor Muscles are Better? You Kinda Don’t…

The Gist -This hot piece of paper comes from a bundle of Dutch and Qatari researchers in JOSPT who have been putting out a long series of adductor articles. Not important, but one of them works for Ajax, the top-level Dutch soccer team, so that’s cool. Anyway, in this specific study they wanted to see if they could figure out where athletes were in the rehab process from acute adductor injuries by assessing strength, flexibility, and palpation pain. Did it work?

In short, not really. They got some interesting data but, even though they took measurements 5 days a week in an intense rehab program, they weren’t able to build the roadmap they were seeking. Here’s some of that interesting data though:

After acute adductor injury, flexibility returns to normal early in rehab, while eccentric adduction strength rapidly increases at first, then increases very slowly for the rest of the program. Palpation pain (which is basically where you poke a patient and see where it makes them say “ouch”) can give a better rough impression of rehab progress than flexibility and strength. The length and width of palpation pain decreased in a near-linear fashion and petered out about when the athletes were passing some return to play metrics.

Tell Me More -Why does this matter? Wouldn’t it be nice to be able to say, “You’re about 75% of the way back”? Yea, I thought so. That’s what the researchers were pursuing, but they didn’t quite get there. The rehab protocol was locked down and testing happened almost every day, meaning they had a great shot at putting this together from their pool of 61 athletes, but still didn’t make it. Possibly the best information we got from this research was what didn’t indicate recovery track.

In hamstring injury, some research has shown that flexibility recovery is a good method to describe rehab progress, but adductors don’t follow that pattern. Because of the relatively erratic strength gains, it doesn’t provide a good roadmap either.

Lastly, the whole goal was to check where athletes were on the return to sport scale, so they needed return to sport milestones. They defined those in another in this paper series, so check that one out if you’re interested.

Where’s this paper though?It’s right here, as always. You really should know that by now 😞. Not open access, but APTA members can get it since it’s JOSPT.

Ever Heard of Carpal Stabilizing Taping? It Can Reduce Pain. Cool!

The Gist -Researchers publishing in the Journal of Hand Therapy did an RCT to see if they could reduce weight-bearing wrist pain via carpal stabilization taping. And they did! They used rigid (ie, not stretchy, not like made of metal or something) tape around the proximal carpal bones in one group and stretchy, Kinesio Tape-style tape on the other group and put them through six sessions of weight bearing wrist movement across one week. Subjects grabbed a table with their affected hand and used it to push themselves up into a standing position.

After the intervention, the 15 people in the intervention group increased AROM by 8.6° and PROM by 6.8° over the other group, and decreased their VAS-measured pain by 1.8 cm. Also interestingly, this wasn’t performed by hand therapists, but by orthopedic therapists. An interesting, simple mechanism to reduce pain.

Tell Me More -Why does this work? Unlike when we usually ask that question, we actually have good information here. The tape reduces unwanted joint motion, specifically restricting midcarpal joint extension and supination/pronation of proximal carpal bones, specifically the relationship between the lunate and the scaphoid. This restriction encourages further extension at the radiocarpal joint, rather than the midcarpal. According to the paper, this improves congruity in the radoiocarpal joint and improves palmar gliding of the proximal carpal bones.

The study is not without limitations, as it was quite small (30 subjects), and excluded some people with some common dorsal wrist problems, reducing applicability. It was also a short trial (1 week), so we’re not yet sure about the long-term outcomes. Lastly, the kinematic data above is suppositional, they didn’t take any scans or 3D data to back it up. Still, rigid taping like this could be used to increased wrist ROM and decrease pain, though it’s early in the process.

Paper?Yupp, yupp, yupp.

Be Conservative to Reduce Fear in Chronic LBP

The Gist -Our last piece comes from the Archives of PM&R and covers a systematic review of 61 RCTs related to fear and kinesiophobia in chronic LBP. The goal was to find interventions that reduced this, and they were marginally successful. The title says it all, conservative interventions reduce fear. It’s a really complex study, but here are some highlights:
- Moderate evidence that multidisciplinary interventions reduce kinesophobia
- No between group differences for manual, electro, K tape, or craniosacral therapy
- Psychological interventions (CBT and the like) were effective in reducing kinesiophobia
- Surgery did not reduce fear-avoidance beliefs
- Exercise significantly reduced kinesiophobia compared to control and reduced fear-avoidance beliefs

Tell Me More -I think we can safely say that physical therapy in general reduced fear in the patients assessed in these studies. Exercise programs were most effective, while modalities didn’t show any between-group differences, surgery did not help. The researchers point out that fear can limit the effects of conservative interventions if it is not specifically addressed in treatment and encourage providers to assess and treat fear in their practice. They also recommend training for physical therapists on psychological interventions in order to make the most of their treatment. They go much deeper into the psychological elements, but it’s much too much to summarize here. Check out the full paper for that information.

I know that this paper kind of boils down to “Physical Therapy works.” That’s great news, but not super clinically useful, I know. The best area to use this information is probably in discussion with patients or physicians. It’s really good data to show that surgery won’t magically change someone’s life, but PT possibly could.
