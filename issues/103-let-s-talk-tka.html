How to intervene for TKA in Acute Care

The Gist -This systematic review from the Journal of Acute Care PT tried to better understand what the best approach to TKA rehab in the acute setting would be. They looked into research all the way from 1996 to 2016 where people received PT within 7 days of TKA surgery. Overall, they found 40 studies to digest that ran the gamut from “additional” rehab to “early” rehab to Chinese medicine, CPM, Biofeedback, and everything in between. I’m not going to break all of them down, but the most relevant ones are probably early rehab, “PT Interventions”, and cryotherapy.

The 5 studies of early rehab, of which 2 were RCTs, showed low quality evidence that beginning rehab within 48 hours of surgery improves knee ROM and strength and decreases length of stay.

“PT Interventions” is a big bucket that they put 31 studies into. All of these combines “some form of ‘physical therapy’ or exercise intervention with the intervention under study.” Many of these studies didn’t do well at actually stating the type, intensity, and duration of PT interventions, though 27 included gait, 14 transfer, and 22 ROM. In general, these studies demonstrated effectiveness, though their heterogeneity was very high.

Lastly, cryotherapy was the only intervention with significant enough evidence to say that it reduced pain at higher than a low level of evidence.

Tell Me More -This big study is kind of disappointing really, but there’s a good reason for that. There’s not enough good research in this area. There’s a fair bit of research, but much of it is at a high risk of bias or has other problems. According to the authors, “the lack of evidence for the effectiveness of most of the studied interventions is due to similar management of the intervention and comparison groups, and that changes in the outcomes studied are largely effected by various forms of interventions suggested by the term ‘physical therapy’ or ‘standard care.’ In addition, all study participants likely received medical pain management of some type.”

Because of this, we’re left with some very general information they summarize well:

For patients with TKA in the acute care setting, very low-level evidence supports (1) cryotherapy early in the postoperative phase for reducing pain over other physical therapy interventions; (2) early, aggressive mobility interventions for improving ROM and walk- ing ability rather than less aggressive approach; and (3) neurostimulation for reduction of pain over placebo or no neurostimulation. However, the effect of these interventions is uncertain due to the limitations of the studies.
The authors go on to say that we can only really rely on our clinical judgment for issues beyond what is in the block quote above. Sad, but true.

Did you test function? You’d better.

The Gist -This perspective piece in PTJ argues for testing more than just ROM and patient-reported outcome measures in TKA. This bundle of PTs from all over the country came together to emphasize that we need functional testing to make sure that people are getting better. They argue that “muscle strength is the rate-limited step for recovery of function after TKA,” so we need to be testing muscles. “during the first two weeks following TKA, profound strength loss occurs throughout the lower extremities and especially in the quadriceps. Strength loss approaching or exceeding 50% occurs in both the quadriceps and hamstrings approximately 1 month after TKA.” And since quads weakness influences functional recovery, we need to strengthen the quads. But how can we make sure we’re actually doing that without testing it out? We can’t. So we need functional tests.

They believe that the best collection of outcome measures includes knee extension and flexion ROM, quads and hamstrings strength testing, the pain scale and the KOOS JR outcome measure, and the 30 second STS, the TUG, and the 4-meter walk test.

I’ll finish their approach to strength here and move on to other topics in the next section with this fantastic block quote:

We recommend testing quadriceps and hamstring strength in both the involved (surgical) and contralateral limbs preoperatively and regularly throughout the postoperative recovery period and beyond. Quadriceps strength and activation may be evaluated early after surgery during the performance of isometric quadriceps contractions (assess for superior glide of the patella) and straight leg raises (assess for the presence of an extension lag). After patients can perform a straight leg raise without a lag, more formal isometric strength testing should begin, typically around 3 weeks postoperatively, although “tolerance criteria” and individual healing timeframes should also be considered. Strength testing should be conducted at least every few weeks or when there is a change in patient status during the rehabilitation period and at follow-up visits after formal rehabilitation.

Tell Me More -They go on to demonstrate how focusing on strength outcomes doesn’t compromise ROM, so we should do it. And, according to research they cite, “neither knee flexion nor knee extension ROM meaningfully predict performance-based functional outcomes at medium- or long- term follow-up after TKA”, so it’s not enough.

Instead of just strength and ROM, you need objective measurements of function.“Performance-based functional tests are superior to ROM and PROs at predicting function after TKA. Health care providers should use objective, functional performance-based tests as opposed to ROM and/or PROs alone to evaluate function before and after TKA. Several valid and reliable functional tests—such as the TUG, 30s sit to stand chair test, and 4-Meter Walk Test—require few resources (eg, chair and/or hallway), little time (ie, \<5 minutes), and no special equipment or training.”

Especially in a world where research has come out saying that physical therapy after TKA may not be needed because people can gain their ROM back on their own, this is an important argument to make. Multiple recent studies have attempted to demonstrate that people can recover from TKA on their own but none of them include functional testing, making them poor predictors. Still, the authors argue, if we don’t use functional testing, we’re doing just as poorly. So do the TUG, the 30sSTS and the 4-m WT, and find out how your patients are really doing.

Getting a joint replacement doesn’t make you more active. Sorry.

The Gist - One would assume that getting a knee replacement would let you “get back in the game” like all the advertisements say. Unfortunately, that’s not usually the case. This article in PTJ looked into the long-term physical activity metrics of people who had a total joint arthroplasty and found that getting a new joint was not associated with either moderate or vigorous physical activity. They grabbed a dataset of 55 or older community-dwelling adults who had symptomatic OA and needed joint replacements. Some got them, some didn’t, which let them compare these 4,652 people in the dataset.

The outcome they looked at was self-reported physical activity. At baseline, 90.6% of the population reported participating in vigorous activity while 71.2% said they participated in moderate activity once per week or less. 80.7% and 44.5%, respectively, didn’t participate in these activities at all. In the two years after TJA, physical activity decreased in every population. It didn’t decrease a ton, but it didn’t increase either, even though the pain from the diseased joint was now (probably) gone.

Tell Me More -I’ll let the authors drill down to the real point:

Though people generally report improvement in pain and function after TJA, continued inactivity indicates current rehabilitation protocols may not be sufficient to address physical inactivity in patients after TJA. After TJA, patients typically enter rehabilitation services for the treatment of functional limitations. Currently, standard TJA postoperative physical therapy protocols effectively address physical functions such as strength, gait, and transfer training. Despite evidence that PA can negate chronic disease progression after TJA, increased PA is not a standard rehabilitation goal after surgery. Clinicians in general and physical therapists in particular commonly assume passive roles with respect to patient PA outcomes.

They go on to argue how we as PTs need to specifically promote physical activity alongside rehabilitation. Not only do we need to discuss activity with patients and promote it, we also need to understand how it is linked to other contexts and improve those: “PA specifically is associated with environmental contexts such as socioeconomic status, social cohesion, access to health care and education, and discrimination. Because PA is associated with context, successful intervention on PA in post-TJA populations requires clinician knowledge of local risk factors for physical inactivity (eg, Is the community walkable? Does the patient have exercise support?)”

I’m not goin to harp on about environmental contexts like walkable neighborhoods, because y’all know how important that is to me. I’ll just finish by saying that the elements that lead to TJA are complex, as are the elements of life coming out of TJA, so a more complete rehabilitative program is required to help promote physical activity at every stage.
