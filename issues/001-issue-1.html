PT CRAB Issue #1 - Hand Mobes, Weight Loss for MSK Pain, and Post-Stroke Exercise

This week, we have reviews and studies that run the gamut from full body to small parts. JOSPT publishes about weight-loss interventions for pain reduction in MSK disorders (and the results surprise), Archives of PM&R has a big systematic review on exercise in post stroke patients, and the Journal of Physiotherapy has advice on using mobilization to heal distal radial fractures.
Let's dive in.
New here? Below, you'll find a summary of recent research to help you become a better clinician, with links to the full articles. More details for newbies at the bottom!

Weight Loss Doesn't Help Knee and Hip OA

A systematic review and meta-analysis from JOSPT shows that weight loss may not help hip and knee OA as much as we'd think. Nor spinal pain neither. The researchers grabbed 22 trials with 3600 participants and dug into the data. The results were a bit surprising, at least for me.

The Gist - They found low evidence that weight loss interventions helped with pain and disability, compared to minimal care in PT. In Knee OA specifically, weight-loss interventions were not more effective than exercise only for pain and disability. Weight loss could help in many areas of life, but it looks like Hip and Knee OA aren't those.

I Want More - Excess weight is often blamed for OA, and people who are overweight or obese have 3x increase knee OA, according to the paper. Because of this, weight loss is often recommended for treatment. The researchers did a comprehensive synthesis of all weight loss interventions, hoping to capture all data possible on it. They grabbed a lot. Here are the highlights.
* Weight loss vs. minimal intervention for pain: \<1 level change on 1 - 10 paint scale
* Weight loss vs. minimal intervention for disability: small effect (-3.7pts on WOMAC)
* Weight loss vs. minimal intervention for weight: Large effect! So, that's good.
* Weight loss vs. Exercise only: No difference on pain or disability. Small difference on weight loss.
* Diet and exercise vs. exercise only: Small effect on pain, disability, and weight loss.

Looks like weight loss helps you lose weight. That's about all the study found. It doesn't help with OA or LBP treatment. It could prevent those (the researchers don't comment on this), but won't really help them.

Alright, gimme the paper. Done. (https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2020.9041)

How to Exercise Post-Stroke Patients

You're probably already exercising your post-stroke patients pretty hard, but here's some data and guidelines to back up and tweak what you're doing. A review and meta-analysis from the Archives of PM&R showed great news from 10 articles reporting 8 different trials. These trials all followed American Stroke Association recommendations for exercise (https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/full/10.1161/str.0000000000000022) : Aerobic 3-5 times/wk for 20-60min at moderate intensity and Resistance 2-3 times/wk with 1-3 sets of 10-15 reps of large muscle groups (squats, deadlifts, etc) at 50-80% of 1-RM. (Write these down, seriously. They're harder to find that you'd think.)

The Gist - These interventions increased habitual walking speed and walking endurance and had moderate-level evidence that they increased TUG times as well. Combining aerobic and resistance training definitely has an effect. The researchers note that most clinicians under-prescribe aerobic exercise or fail to combine aerobic and resistance, factors that could reduce recovery outcomes. So, be sure to prescribe enough aerobic training to stress the cardiovascular system. Don't skimp! Adapt regularly, following the FITT principle.

I Want More - Let's start with some honesty. The effects weren't huge. That doesn't mean they're not useful, but they're not huge. Walking endurance was a difference of 40m in a 6 minute walk test, and speed was 0.07m/s (.15mph), which isn't a lot. But that doesn't mean they're not meaningful. The researchers looked at a few other outcome measures and spotted very small differences, mostly because they weren't really tested. Sit to Stand, Stair Climb, and overall Physical Activity Levels didn't show large enough differences to be significant. A commentary on the paper (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1836955320300059?via%3Dihub) points out that the average exercise trial started 25 months post-stroke. Yikes. That's a long time. We definitely need more research on all this, but we know what is important and what does help. Exercise. Aerobic and Resistance. As soon after stroke as possible. Following the FITT principle and pushing them hard (within reason)

Alright, Gimme the Paper. Gotcha (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31400308/)

Mobs for Wrist Rehab - Move that Radius!

An RCT of 67 patients with distal radius fracture that had been treated with casting (not surgery) tried to figure out if mobilization with movement was better than exercises and advice. They added MWM to the exercise and advice to see how things differed between groups.

The Gist - They showed that adding supination and extension mobilization with movement improved supination, extension, and flexion ROMs, and improved scores on QuickDASH and PRWE at 4 weeks. It also improved ROMs at 12 weeks, though QuickDASH and PRWE scores were not significantly different at 12. The MWM didn't show increased benefit at 26 or 52 weeks, so it looks like this brings faster benefits, not larger ones. Details on the exercises and MWM they did are in appendix 2 and 3 of the study, here. (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1836955320300278#sectitle0155)

I Want More - All patients were given 4 PT sessions in 4 weeks, and an HEP to do 2x per day. The intervention group also got MWM in supination and extension from the PT and instructions on how to do it themselves twice per day. The supination outcome measure was most important to them, since it's usually most limited, and they picked extension, strength, flexion, pronation, and the QuickDASH (http://www.dash.iwh.on.ca/sites/dash/public/translations/QuickDASH_English_Australia.pdf) , PRWE (https://www.physio-pedia.com/PRWE_Score) , and QoL short form (https://www.physio-pedia.com/12-Item_Short_Form_Survey_(SF-12)) as secondary measures. They started all interventions within 9 days of cast removal. Good news! They worked. The treatment sped up recovery of function and ROM by most of their measurements, and the measurements all came together after six months. It didn't improve strength, pronation, or the QoL.

Alright, gimme the paper. Here ya go. (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1836955320300278)
