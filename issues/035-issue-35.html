DN Helps OA, At Least for a Bit

The Gist -Researchers wanted to know what effects dry needling could have on people suffering from OA, so they did a fantastic trial! This double-blind, placebo controlled RCT sought to investigate the short-term effects of dry needling on physical function, pain, and hip muscle strength in people with hip OA. They split their patients into a sham group and real, gave them three treatment sessions where they stabbed some myofascial trigger points, then ran some outcome measures.

Here’s what they found: The dry needling significantly improved patient’s isometric strength, reduced their pain intensity (by 3 points!), and increased their functional abilities. They were faster on the TUG and the 40m walk test and got better scores on the WOMAC too. Yay!

Tell Me More -Here are some more details and some downsides. On the detail front, they applied the dry needling treatments in patients’ iliopsoas, rectus femoris, tensor fasciae latae, and gluteus minimus muscles, stabbing in those all around the hip. You’ll note that these are all hip intra-articular pressure increasing muscles, well-done researchers. On the downside, this was decidedly a short-term study. Patients were measured 48 hours after DN (or sham), so we don’t know what effects this has on the long-term disease state.

You Probably Can’t Do Running Analysis with Your Smartphone. Sorry.

The Gist -Researchers publishing in the journal of Physical Therapy in Sport wanted to find out how effective the Hudl Technique app is in analyzing runners with PFP pain. Unfortunately, the results were poor. They compared markerless, smartphone analysis to standard, 3D kinematic analysis, having 21 runners run on a treadmill while the Hudl people and the 3D kinematics system watched. They used peak knee flexion and peak hip adduction as their measurements to compare.

It was rough. The 2D validity (measured via ICC) was only 0.42 for Knee flexion and 0.06 for Hip adduction. Inter-rater reliability? That was just 0.31 for Hip adduction and a better (but still not great) 0.71 for Knee flexion. Ouch.

Tell Me More -Even though the headline says it, I’m not saying you can’t do anything from the Hudl technique gait analysis in the clinic. We just don’t know. Comparing a state of the art 3d kinematic system to a smartphone app is a bit underhanded, but hey, it’s the gold standard so we gotta work from somewhere. It’s not all bad news as well. The researchers reference an earlier study that showed good results using 2D, markerless analysis to do a similar thing, but those researchers used a large computer screen to measure rather than an iPad. This team speculates that the form factor of the Hudl Technique app may be what held back their results.

If you’re interested in gait analysis, the paper’s discussion section (usually the best section of any paper) is loaded with information about other studies and gait analysis in general. Definitely check it out for more.

Lumbar Fusion? Try Neural Mobilization, Or Myofascial Release. They Both Work!

The Gist -This paper is a rare double good news edition coming from the Archives of PM&R. Researchers wanted to know what the best rehab after lumbar fusion was, so they popped patients into a trial. 60 patients, three groups: stabilization exercises only, stabilization plus myofascial release, and stabilization plus neural mobilization. Unsurprisingly, patients in all 3 groups got better, but myofascial release + stabilization got better better than stabilization alone and neural mobilization + stabilization got better best!

They checked after 1 month of treatment using the Oswestry, VAS pain scale, and back range of motion as outcome measures. They found no difference in ROM between groups, but significant ones in the Oswestry and pain, with the neural mobilization group improving 20mm on pain versus stabilization alone and myofascial release improving by 11mm.

Tell Me More -The goal was to further elucidate the best approach after lumbar fusion, since that’s still up in the air even as the number of fusions per year rises. These researchers looked into people who were between 35 and 50 years old with a primary diagnosis of degenerative disk disease. They all received 3 exercise sessions per week for 4 weeks, while one group received neural mobilization (AKA nerve glides) of their sciatic nerves by adding traction to an SLR and another received myofascial release to their thoracolumbar fascia, QL, piriformis, and hamstrings in the form of a hands-applied stretch.

For more details, read the paper! Archives of PM&R usually publishes good, readable papers, so enjoy it.

Walk Everywhere in SCI Rehab.

The Gist -In SCI rehab, avoiding falls is huge, right? So how can we help patients avoid more falls? Get them walking on weird surfaces, that’s how. This study was carried out in tertiary rehabilitation centers as patients went through gait training on a standard track compared to training on a track with different surfaces (including artificial turf, soft rubber, and pebbles). They split 54 patients into control and experimental, then put them through gait training for 30 minutes per day, 5 days per week for 4 weeks either on weird surfaces or the standard, based on the group. It went swimmingly!

Patients in the experimental group showed significant 6MWT, TUG, and Sit-To-Stand improvement at 2 and 4 weeks while those in control did not. At 6 months, only 18% of the experimental group had taken a fall compared with 46% of control. Noice!

Tell Me More -I will do so gladly. They created artificial environments for their patients out of 10 meter long x 1 meter wide tracts of fake pebbles, 1.5inch artificial turf, and 3inch foam rubber. The participants practiced on these surfaces daily, taking turns walking on each one. This improved their ability at the end of the month and through the first 6 months, but, unfortunately, the results did not last beyond 6 months, with the experimental group returning to baseline abilities after 5 months without this type of training. Researchers speculate that this is due to the lack of a novel task to learn from, so participants regressed.
