Prognosis, ever again

But back to business, I’m going to open with a paper from PTJ this year that looks into prognostic models and discusses the 6 that the writers say have been externally validated. After that, we’ll do some short scenes of some of models so you can learn about them and a little bit about what to do with them. Clinically Valuable Models

The Gist -Look y’all, I’m not qualified to comment upon the quality of this paper. Or really, many papers. And you know that’s not what I do. I think traditional journal clubs are dumb and am happy to expound upon that thought if you shoot me an email (Luke@PTCrab.org). Instead, I trust the journals and the authors. And boy does this have a good collection of authors including Yannick Tousignant-Laflamme, Florian Nye, Annie LeBlanc, and Chad E. “Daddy” Cook (as in “Daddy of Excellent PT Research”) and more. And it’s from PTJ. All this is to say that I trust their critical appraisal, which is good because this is a complex article. Fortunately, it has a simple conclusion, so let’s get straight to it.

This is a systematic review of prognostic tools for MSK conditions. The goal is to predict patient health outcomes in rehab and they read 300 full text articles, eventually including 46 papers on 37 distinct models. And after all that, they found 6 models that have external validation: 1) STart Back Screening Tool, 2) Wallis Occupational Rehabilitation RisK mode, 3) Da Silva model 4) PICKUP model, 5) Schellingerhout rule, and 6) Keene model. The writers concluded that these models were clinically relevant and externally validated for predicting prognosis with a few different conditions. I’m going to go into those models more below, but first, a few more details.

STart is a tool for screening back problems and categorizing people into low, medium, or high risk of pain sticking around for a while. It’s 9 items and quick to do. The Schellingerhout clinical prediction rule predicts whether someone with non-specific neck pain will still have it 6 months later. The Wallis Occupational Rehabilitation RisK tool (AKA WORRK) predicts return to work after occupational trauma. Da Silva is about predicting the number of days until pain recovery for people with acute low back pain. The Keene model is about recurrent lateral ankle sprains. Lastly the PICKUP model predicts health-related quality of life and work ability between 11-27 months after the beginning of acute/subacute low back of neck pain.

Tell Me More -This entire paper revolved around the PROBAST, a new tool to asses risk of bias in prognostic studies from the Cochrane Prognosis Methods Group. The goal of the tool is to explore risk of bias and applicability to the clinic in addition to participants, predictors, and outcomes. But it doesn’t assess whether or not a tool is impactful upon clinical practice. To find that, they had a five step process. The model had to have low concerns about applicability (from the PROBAST tool), a complete report of performance measures, good or acceptable calibration, good discrimination, and relatively low risks of bias. And if that’s all gobbledy-gook, I get it. It’s not much better to me too, that’s why I read researchers and journals that I trust.

Paper?Sure, but I think you’ll get more value from the next bit where we break down a few of those models

First, Schellingerhout on Necks

The Gist -This paper reviewed three RCTs from the Netherlands with 468 patients who were assigned to a GP, physical therapy, manual therapy, or an activity program. But that wasn’t the point. The point that the researchers looked into were potential predictors of ongoing pain. 6 months later. They looked at 17 variables including age, gender, employment status, cause of pain, headache, LBP, NDI, fear of movement, and more, then did lots of fancy modeling to see what was what.

Overall, 43% of people reported persistent pain complaints 6 months after initial presentation. Which is like, a lot and it establishes our baseline. Meanwhile, 57% didn’t and there were clear differences between groups. Clear enough that the researchers were able to come up with a scoring system.

In the scoring system, people with 35 points or more had a significantly higher risk of neck pain 6 months later than those with less. Specifically, scores of 35-54 had a 40-60% probability of neck pain at 6 months, scores 55-79 had a 60-80% probability, and scores less than 10 had a 0-20% probability. Other scores fill in those gaps.

Tell Me More -So how do you get points? Here goes:
- Age - +7 points per 10 years of age
- Accompanying low back pain - +21 points
- Traumatic cause - +6 points
- Health status - Negative 3 points per 25 points of improved status
- Accompanying headache - +5 points
- Radiation of pain to elbow/shoulder - Negative 14 points
- Previous neck complaints - +13 points
- Paid employment - +9 points
- Pain intensity scale - Negative 1 point per point of initial pain
- Radiation to elbow/shoulder - Negative 4 points
- Previous neck complaints - +4 points
- Paid employment - Negative 11 points

Keep in mind, this is the likelihood of persistent non-specific neck pain at 6 months later. It’s better than guessing, but it doesn’t tell you anything about how long after 6 months things may resolve, so keep that in mind.

Lastly, the sample of 468 people let them make a fairly good analysis of the sensitivity and specificity of the scale as well as the positive predictive value and if you’re a real nerd, that’s all available in the paper. And actually lastly, they couldn’t include any physical characteristics in the model, nor the TSK because they weren’t taken in 2 and 1 of the RCTs they reviewed, respectively.

Paper? Yupp.

PICKUP this model for back pain

The Gist -Did you know that “Low back pain is a major health problem”? Boy do I hate when papers start like that. But this is actually a good paper. They exported 2,758 patient records of folks who had acute LBP to build a prognostic model of who would get chronic (pain at 3 months). The model was about 68% predictive of who had pain 3 months later when they validated it, so not too bad! The goal is to figure out which patients are high risk for persistent pain. This could be more helpful for primary care settings (PT should be primary care! But I digress) but it’s interesting and potentially useful for you too. Let’s see what’s what.

The score relies on 5 questions with variable scoring. First, you’ve got “How much low back pain have you had during the past week?” On a 1-6 scale, then “Do you have leg pain?” 0 is no, 1 is yes, next is the question of whether worker’s comp or something like that is paying for treatment again 0 is no, 1 is yes, then we have “How much have you been bothered by feeling depressed in the past week?” On a 0-10 scale, and, finally, “In your view, how large is the risk that you current pain may become persistent?” On a 0-10 scale too. Then you do a bunch of math.

Score = -0.55+0.89x(-2.82 + (0.21xPain + 0.44xLeg + 0.50xComp + 0.06xDepress + 0.13xRisk))

And if that seems dumb, that’s because it is. But we’re not done!

To get the probability you divide this score by the score plus 1 to get the probability.

But what’s the point if you’re not going to do the math? Hang on.

Tell Me More -I know you’re not going to ask the exact questions and do the math. I just know you. But that’s okay, because the coefficients tell us a lot. The math is just for a clean prediction and probability. And if you want that, you can get it. But let’s pay attention to the relative weights of each factor. Based on those, you can see that leg pain is about twice as predictive as overall pain, depression is 1/3rd as predictive as pain, worker’s comp is more predictive than leg pain, and the patient’s feelings about the risk of future pain is a bit more than half as predictive as current pain and twice as predictive as having depression.

Does that make sense? It comes down to the difference in weight each has in the model. And technically, knowing this doesn’t predict anything about individual patients and breaking out pieces of the model is risky and removes some validity, I know. But if you want something clinically useful for yourself from this, that’s it.

Paper?Yuppp yupp yup.
