Those Weird IT Bands

This week, we’re blowing up what we know about ITBS and upright tilting in a hospital beds too. It’s time to rethink what you think. Also this week, variability in walking performance and rhabdomyolysis after using a massage gun. Yikes.

Let’s dive in!

Using Walking Performance as An Outcome Measure? Be Careful.

The Gist -This interesting paper from the Journal of Neurologic PT looked at the daily walking of people post-stroke and those with Parkinson’s disease and found some remarkable stuff. Mean steps per day between the two groups fit previous data, but, interestingly, more walking per day was related to more day-to-day gait variability, muddying up measured walking performance. Hmmm… Fastest walking speed and self-selected walking speed were not correlated to steps per day, but variability in those metrics were. The more people walked, the more their walking performance on tests varied each day. When you’re collecting this and other gait data, get your baseline more than once to screen this out. The standard in clinical research is 3 times, apparently.

Tell Me More -84 individuals gave data to the study, so the results are fairly robust. Overall, the results show that the more someone is walking (which is good!), the more variable their walking performance is day-to-day (which is annoying). This data was collected on people with PD and post-stroke but, interestingly, variability was independent of diagnosis. It was only related to their daily amount of walking. They ran a lot of models and found some other interesting stuff too:
- People who lived in areas of lower socioeconomic status had more variability in their amounts of walking each day
- Walking capacity did not affect day-to-day variability
- Gait speed was moderately correlated with steps per day
Overall, the study shows that those who walk more are the ones to watch out for when taking walking performance data. They’ll have the most day-to-day variability in walking dynamics, while those who spend less time up and about are easier to measure just once in the clinic.

IT Bands Don’t Work Like We Thought

The Gist -O.o - That about sums up this juicy bit of research from the Journal of Physical Therapy in Sport. Researchers from Austria used a new type of ultrasound, shear wave elastography, to figure out what IT band tone looks like in runners with IT Band Syndrome and compare it to non-runners and themselves after treatment for their problems. Pre-treatment, there were no differences detected between the IT band characteristics and hip muscle strength between the injured runners and healthy controls. The injured went through a 2 week period of manual therapy followed by 4 weeks of neuromuscular education, strengthening exercises of their hips and stretching and foam rolling of their IT bands, glutes, and TFLs. After all this, the runners got way better (average pain down from 7.8 (ow!) to 1.7), but their IT bands got stiffer, not longer and more flexible like the researchers predicted. Weird.

Tell Me More -This requires elucidation, I know. According to the paper, the major theory behind ITBS is that it’s an impingement syndrome caused by cysts, bursae, or fat pads underneath the IT band being compressed by a hypertonic band, causing inflammation and pain. That’s why the researchers tried to measure IT band tension before and after rehab. Even though these patients got way better (down 6.1 pain, up 17% on the LEFS, and 50% reported being pain free), their IT bands got stiffer. According to the standard theory, they should have felt worse. Weird. This throws a shadow over that theory, though it’s not too dark a shadow yet. Things are still early. Here’s why:
- The study was only 14 people. Yea, not great.
- The runners were told not to run for 6 weeks which could have prevented inflammation from the IT band rubbing around down there.
- Shear wave elastography is still young and we’re not sure of its test-retest reliability yet.
Still, this is new, interesting information and I hope the researchers expand this study in the future.

Rhabdomyolysis after Massage Gun. Yikes.

The Gist -This case report is the first published case of an adverse event related to a massage gun and boy is it a scary one. Published by a group of researchers from China, the report details the experience of a 25 year old woman who received 10 minutes of percussive massage from a commercial massage gun and presented with rhabdomyolysis the next day. She didn’t have any risk factors for rhabdo before and was generally healthy except for iron deficient anemia (something not formerly associated with rhabdomyolysis). Fortunately, she reported to the hospital quickly after observing a color change in her urine, was treated successfully and returned to regular life a few days later.

Tell Me More -Neither I nor the paper is saying that this is a common risk of percussive massage guns, but it is an important report since it’s the first of its kind. Rhabdomyolysis is pretty rare, with just 26,000 cases in the U.S. annually. Most of these are due to muscle trauma; something which the researchers speculate was the problem here. In the course of the paper the researchers went into all the different known causes for rhabdo, including traditional Chinese medicine, some pharmaceuticals, previous infections, diabetes mellitus, illicit drugs, wasp stings, and more, introducing them as to screen them out for this patient. Based on her history, this was probably a trauma case caused by the massage gun. So keep your eyes on this space in the next few years and we’ll see if anything else adverse occurs with your theraguns.

Upright Tilting Probably Does Nothing

The Gist - We’ve got another O.o paper here. These researchers looked into the common practice of upright tilting in a hospital bed to elicit muscle activation in bed-bound adults. They tested in a health population, popping EMG sensors onto their Rec. Fems, Tib. Ants, Erector Spinal, and Glute Meds and then tilting them up from 10° to 80° either while strapped into bed or not. There are a few reasons to tilt someone near upright as part of a rehab process, but these researchers were specifically looking into muscle activation. Here’s what they found.

EMG activation in all those muscles was minimal throughout the entire arc of tilting, from 10° to 80°. With straps removed, they did get slightly higher muscle activation out of the Rec. Fems from 40° to 80°, but it was very slight. Just above minimum detectable change with their EMG software. The researchers say that the concept should be explored more to see whether knee and hip angles and strap locations could be changed to increase muscle activation, but the standard approach doesn’t seem to do it.

Tell Me More - This was all done via surface EMG. It’s not the most detailed EMG analysis possible but that’s okay in this case as the researchers were looking at gross muscle activation and didn’t really find any. The only big activation noted was in Tib. Anterior when the tilting process began. But after 10°, this leveled off to baseline.

Worryingly, in the discussion section, researchers also noted that early mobilization does not regularly happen in critical care (based on four recent studies), but that’s kind of an aside from the paper itself. All data points to early exercise being key to functional recovery so please, please do it if possible.

Lastly, limitations? Yea, there are some. It was a small study done on healthy people, not on people with critical illness in the ICU. Still, it’s unlikely that their muscles work very differently from one another. The researchers also didn’t do blinding and randomization, so maybe a future study can do that to get more robust results. Still, very interesting data overall.
