If You Want to Get FIIT, HIIT May Not Be Best Actually

The Gist -Okay, that headline isn’t specifically true, this only applies to women with fibromyalgia using the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire as a primary outcome, and the VAS scale for pain, the SF-36, and a cardiopulmonary exercise test as secondaries. They popped 55 people into a study, splitting them into High Intensity Interval Training, Medium Intensity Continuous Training, and Control Groups. They did what you would expect, with HIIT doing four sets of 4 minute high intensity intervals interspersed with 3 sets of recovery and MICT doing 45 minutes of moderate exercise. Afterward, they did 15 minutes of the same strength and stretching exercises. This happened for 5 sessions/wk for 6 weeks.

Afterward, they grabbed a bunch of data and here’s what it said: MICT and HIIT were both significantly better than control across all outcome measures. Yay! MICT and HIIT tied on the Fibromyalgia scale, the SF-36, and cardiopulmonary test. MICT won on other measures, specifically doing better at body weight, fat percentage, fat mass, and body mass. Overall, HIIT and MICT were equal for fibromyalgia, and MICT was better for weight loss.

Tell Me More - Before we do too much more, let’s talk about what fibromyalgia is again, real quick. The diagnostic criteria include a history of generalized pain in at least four regions at a similar level for at least 3 months, widespread pain index of 7 or more and symptom severity score of 5 or more OR widespread pain index of 4-6 and symptom severity score of 9 or more. In other words, you hurt all over at a high level and it sucks.

Other than that, there’s very little complexity in the study. To get specific on the training they did, the aerobic stuff was all on a cycle ergometer while the

Strengthening exercises were performed for 10-minutes, where major muscle groups were strengthened through shoulder press, dumbbell press, shoulder elevation with resistance, biceps curl, squats, hip flexion and extension, and standing hip exercises using 1-3 kg of weight loads and 1 set of 8-10 repetitions. Finally, stretching exercises were performed for 5-minutes by holding the main muscle-tendon groups for 20-30 seconds, with 4-5 repetitions for each muscle group.

The results were a bit surprising, since HIIT has been demonstrated to be more effective at building fitness and cutting fat in a myriad of studies, though not all. Lastly, the authors say that they performed the study due to the prevalence of obesity in patients with fibromyalgia, likely due to their daily pain and symptom severity. Even though it may be tough for them to get started exercising due to these factors, this research shows that they’ll probably be better off and in less pain if they can.

Believe It Or Not, You CAN Test Strength in Telemedicine

The Gist -Rather than from DPTs and MDs, like most of our studies, this one comes from a bundle of physicists from Saint Paul, MN. What better group of people to measure a bunch of Therabands though? Since that’s what they did. They stretched a bunch of green Therabands to calibrate their equipment, then put their volunteers to work pulling them into flexion and extension. Turns out, they could come up with a normalized scale of Theraband length v force of stretch, but the volunteers inserted a new problem, arm length.

They recorded volunteers stretching Therabands and measured their arm angles via a goniometer app pointed at the screen. Volunteers with longer arms had to stretch the Theraband more to get to a given angle, so they also had to produce a higher force. Fortunately, the authors made some sweet charts that can be used to match arm length to angle and come up with the force required to get there. It’s smart stuff. Using their graphs, a measuring tape, a Goni, and a green theraband, you can measure the force of anyone’s shoulder flexion or extension via telemedicine. Just have them measure their arm length (or guess, since men average 66cm and women 59) and stretch the Theraband, then measure the angle of flexion/extension and consult the table to find the force used.

Tell Me More -This is another study without too much complexity, but I do have a bit more. The researchers wanted to make this a stand-in for MMT, but that’s tough to do since there aren’t reference values for shoulder strength and it’s not the same type of motion. Still, for comparing relative strength deficits (affected vs. unaffected), this is a well calibrated method that’s better than “lacks strength”. You can find out exactly how much strength is lacking. This (obviously) works via telemedicine but could certainly work in the clinic too.

Direct Access PT is Better and Cheaper.

The Gist -This systematic review and meta-analysis of direct access PT vs. physician-first access is but another volley in the war to win unfettered direct access in all 50 states (and DC!). The researchers went deep into the database to find comparisons of disability, health care cost, functional outcome improvements and number of PT visits while also digging for any adverse events. They found that cost is lower, number of visits is lower, disability decreases more greatly, and functional outcomes improve more significantly when direct access is used. Take that physician-first model! 😜

In 2012, half of U.S. adults had a diagnosis of a musculoskeletal disorder and it’s unlikely that the number has gone down since. They drive almost 20% of all health care visits, wow.

In pursuit of this systematic review, the researchers were able to find five studies that directly compare direct access and physician-first access and break down each individually in the paper. Their overall conclusions are above, and here are the numbers that go with them:
- PT cost is $242.63 less for direct access
- Total health care cost is $1818.03 less for direct access
- Number of PT visits is 1.01 less for direct access
- No incidents of missed diagnoses or delays in care were attributed to direct access.

Tell Me More -STROBE rated the quality of evidence moderate to good, which is great news for the pooled data from the studies. The effect sizes were small, but definite, and the clinical significance is clear from the numbers above. The reasons for the numbers above are obvious, but why do direct access patients have better functional outcomes? The researchers speculate two things:

Brooks et al suggests that symptom duration reduces efficacy: delays in physical therapy may shift acute episodes of low back pain (when physical therapy is associated with improved outcomes) to subacute or chronic episodes. Denninger et al claims that delay may result in delivery of inappropriate treatments—a common occurrence in traditional medical care models—prior to beginning physical therapy…
Another proposed mechanism for differences is that direct access provides physical therapists the autonomy to implement evidence-based interventions. Physical therapists may prescribe active interventions (eg, exercise and patient education in self-management) while minimizing the use of passive interventions (eg, electrotherapy, ultrasound, and massage) that may be prescribed by physicians on the basis of older models of care. Future research could examine whether greater evidence-based intervention contributes to the decrease in number of physical therapy visits while improving outcomes compared to physician-first access models.
I don’t have room to cover limitations here, but the paper does a great job of breaking them down. Check it out for more. Overall, this is great news that should be shouted from the mountaintops.

Ultramarathons Hurt A Lot

The Gist -In other news, water is wet and tacos are the world’s greatest food (if you’re not watching the Taco Chronicles on Netflix, you’re making a grave error), but I’m done saying incredibly obvious things, let’s get into the data. Tacos are consumed by… Oh right, the PT data. My bad.

This study comes flying out of Brazil and reports on physical therapy interventions during three years of the Ultramarathon Rio 24h, a collection of 602 crazy people running 24 hours of loops in the fourth biggest city in South America. Four PTs were available to work on the runners throughout the race and boy did they. The number of PT care stops was about 3 per runner (1,995 for 602 runners). Only 73 athletes never sought treatment. Most of their intervention were massage, stretching, and cryotherapy, with a few deviating into more intense PT. Unsurprisingly, therapeutic exercise was not used.

At the stops, stretching reduced pain by an average of 2.8 points, massage by 3.2, and cryotherapy by 2.6. The goal was generally to get runners as comfortable as possible as quickly as possible, then pop them back into the race.

Tell Me More -I know there’s not much clinically actionable here, but this kind of stuff is quite interesting to have once in a while. Notably, the times where the highest amount of PT was required were the warmest. This included the afternoons (1:00-6:00pm) and 2014, when it was significantly warmer and more humid than the other two years. There’s not much more to say here, so I’ll leave you with their conclusion and get out of the way:

Massotherapy, cryotherapy, and stretching, per- formed in isolation, have positive effects of decreasing acute musculoskeletal pain in ultramarathon runners.
