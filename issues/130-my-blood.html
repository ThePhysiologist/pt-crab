Issue 130

My Blood?

Last week we talked about hearts, this week we’re staying red and talking blood. Or lack of it, I guess. Yes, it’s blood flow restriction. This has been seen in many gyms for dozens of years, but does it work? Yes, it does. And we’ll talk about how it works here. I go through a bit of the basics, some ideas on how to use it, and even how it could work to improve aerobic capacity in both trained and untrained folks.

When would you use it? For anyone who needs strength gains but has weight-bearing restrictions. Or for improving aerobic capacity. Or for a new, different stimulus. Or just for fun. I use it on folks with meniscus repairs regularly and occasionally with those who lack ankle strength too. If you want to know more, read on. And if you want to know more more, check out some con-ed on it.

Let’s dive in!

What is it and how does it work?

For those who don’t know, BFR has been around for over 50 years, originally reported to be in use by Russian weightlifters. The first appearance in modern scientific literature was 1998 when a group recognized that conditions of blood flow restriction increased muscle strength gains at low loads. I’m not going to get into the details, but to perform BFR, you inflate a variant of a BP cuff over the proximal segment of a limb until blood flow to that area is completely cut off (usually measured via Doppler device), then deflate to a set percentage of that maximal occlusive pressure. After inflating the cuff, there are many different procedures you can go through and I’m definitely not going to get into that here. Check out a con-ed course (including on MedBridge) for details. I’m here to talk about mechanisms.

BFR causes muscle hypertrophy when used without any exercise, during aerobic exercise, and during resistance exercise. But why? Well, we don’t really know. But we have some guesses. It seems like BFR causes additional muscle cell swelling effects and a greater buildup in metabolites since they can’t be flushed away by normal bloodflow. This cell swelling and metabolite buildup causes an increase in local protein synthesis. Low load BFR activates the same cell signaling pathways that high load resistance training activates as long as both are done to failure. This is why BFR doesn’t necessarily increase strength gains in those who can perform high load activities, since they both use the same mechanism.

BFR also has some complex nervous system stuff that I won’t get into here but suffice to say it’s really cool and if you’re into that, definitely check out the paper. But how does this all apply to you? I’m glad you asked, read on.

Tell Me More - It’s important to understand that there’s no particular standard when it comes to BFR training and that’s beacause it’s complicated.

The restrictive stimulus transmitted to the tissue underneath the cuff is influenced by each variable, and as such all should be accounted for to ensure the desired stimulus is being applied as well as to make methodology replicable. Arterial occlusion pressure (the inflation pressure of the cuff required for the cessation of blood flow) in the upper and lower body is dependent upon the width of the cuff; the cessation of blood flow occurs at a lower pressure when cuff width is increased. If the same pressure is applied to an individual using a wide and a narrow cuff, the wide cuff will restrict blood flow to a greater degree.
All of these factors are why you need to take occlusion pressure every time you use BFR, not just once or at the beginning. This is also why companies that don’t recommend taking dopplers and just use absolute pressures (I’m looking at you BStrong) aren’t very useful for BFR. And since “a wide range of restriction (40% to 90% arterial occlusion pressure) pressures seem to be effective for increasing muscle size when exercising with 30% 1RM,” you don’t really have to go up to high pressures. There doesn’t seem to be a huge dose-response relationship with BFR pressure, but there is with reps. We’ll get more into that part later too, so keep your eyes peeled for it.

This paper has a lot more on the technical stuff if that’s your jam. But if you’re looking more for functional use, that’s next.

But What About Runners?

The Gist -Okay, I don’t really know why you asked that question, since it’s something very few people know or ask about, but as long as we’re here, let’s give it a look. Believe it or not, BFR can be used to improve aerobic capacity, VO2max, and lactate threshold in endurance athletes. And non-endurance athletes too. And it may even be able to be done more practically than in the traditional way of using doppler and whatnot (for more on practical applications with adjustable, elastic bands, see this new research here). The big challenge with well-trained endurance athletes is that they’re pretty darn food at endurance athletics. So it’s hard to make them much better. But BFR is a new stimulus that may help improve VO2max and lactate threshold at lower stimulus levels than usual, even in well-trained (VO2max >60!) athletes. Those are people who can run 10Ks in 30-40 minutes, so they’re really fit. Low intensity BFR rowing increased VO2max in those athletes by 9% in 4 weeks. This is nuts. That’s a ton. Seriously. At this level of runner, that’s double the difference between being an Olympics winner and not making Olympic trials. It’s also effective in speeding aerobic gains in recreationally-trained athletes too.

But how is this important clinically? Well remember last week when I talked about the case report on using BFR to improve someone’s stair-climbing ability? It may be possible to combine the aerobic and strength effects of BFR with the correctly designed program. You’ll have to look through a couple of articles and come up with your own ideas, but I’m definitely doing that already.

Tell Me More -This narrative review does look into prescription for endurance athletes a bit, so let’s give it our time. They again reemphasize the importance of not using arbitrary settings: “The pressure for BFR should be set at a fraction of AOP and not arbitrary absolute values that may increase between-subject variability and cause complete arterial occlusion, sub- optimal training stimuli, or thrombosis.” They go on to recommend measuring arterial occlusion pressure in the body position that will be used for the activity (standing for running, sitting on a bike for cycling, etc.) and recommend pressures of about 40-60% of occlusion for no more than 20 minutes, 2-3x/wk. They also note that exercising with cuffs on can be painful.

For intensity, they recommend using perceived exertion since BFR throws off the physiological metrics you’d normally use to predict training stimulus. Of note, “regardless of how exercise intensity is prescribed, it is an important consideration as the combination of high intensities and cuff pressures can reduce sessional exercise volume and minimize physiological adaptations”. Overall, they basically recommend trying high intensity interval training for up to 20 minute periods in addition to standard aerobic exercise. They go on to recommend where to fit this into a periodized training calendar, but you’ll have to read the whole paper for that information.

From the Archive: How Tight Should that Cuff Be? Not as Much as You May Think

This is from Issue 9, possibly the oldest archival piece I’ve featured. That doesn’t mean the info has changed but the style is quite different, jsyk. Though this is quite interesting stuff.
The Gist - We’ll close this week with some occlusion as we talk about what cuff pressure does to blood flow when performing BFR. The researchers involved applied BFR cuffs to the lower limbs of their participants, pumped them up to full occlusion (demonstrated by ultrasound), then checked blood flow through the femoral artery at different pressures, from 0% occlusion all the way up to 100%. Their data was quite interesting.

Bloodflow decreased rapidly from 0% full occlusion pressure up to 40%, then leveled off. It dropped only a tiny bit from 40-80% (from 73mL/min to 61mL/min before dropping rapidly at 90% (to 36mL/min) and 100% (0mL/min).

Tell Me More -This was actually a dissertation from BYU and, as such, the research took place in a university setting. That means the recruits for the intervention were all healthy, early 20’s subjects. Keep that in mind when interpreting the results. The article is over 30 pages long, but really doesn’t pack in any more information than is given above in the gist. It’s an interesting report about the non-linearity of BFR and good to know, since BFR gets a bit more painful with more occlusion, but doesn’t seem to have any extra effect as occlusion increases.

Paper?Okay, but don’t say I didn’t warn you about its length and boringness.
