Issue 154

My head!

This week, we’re talking about what happens after a concussion, well after a concussion. That’s because this week my head is bothering me and I’ve been thinking about it. My last mTBI was four years ago last month and I’m generally doing pretty well, but there are some definite changes present. Most people don’t still have things going on years later, but some do. So let’s talk about post-concussion care with one direct PT thing and two tangential ones.

With that, let’s dive in!

But is it your neck?

The Gist -Here’s the deal folks, it’s possible that some lingering post-concussion symptoms aren’t actually your head. Like headaches, dizziness, and neck pain, for example. Those could all be coming from a neck, but it’s hard to tell. This piece in a 2019 JOSPT edition looks into this. 749 cases of mTBI are estimated to occur per 100,000 person-years and about 48% “report substantial persistent symptoms 1 year following injury.” But if they’re the three symptoms from above, they may not be coming from the brain at all. But how can you tell?

Well, you can’t. But the authors of this paper took a good approach, assessing 20 individuals with concussion and neck pain and putting about half of them (11) through neck treatment with pre and post treatment assessment. The theory was that if treating the neck symptoms improved the “concussion” symptoms then they were probably coming from the neck. Participants were evalled at a mean of 7.5 weeks post-concussion, all had headaches with a mean frequency of 5.6 days per week and 15 of the 20 reported dizziness.

The PTs in the study went their own ways with neck treatment, then assessed at the end. After treatment for neck problems, participants’ headache duration and severity decreased significantly, while dizziness frequency decreased as well.

Tell Me More -This is (obviously) a small study without a lot of controls. It’s also a case-control study, so not the best to draw conclusions from. I know all this to be true. It’s early days on a process like this and there may not be a better way to separate brain symptoms from neck symptoms, but the world is open to ideas. The point to take away is that we shouldn’t rule out either in our approach. There’s no reason to assume that headaches and dizziness are only coming from the brain if there is concurrent neck pain after a concussion. And there is almost always concurrent neck pain from a concussion.

I’ll close with this from the paper

The extent of neck involvement relative to other variables is unclear. Participants who did not achieve resolution of headache or dizziness reported changes in frequency, duration, or severity, which may be clinically relevant. These preliminary results suggest that neck treatment is worth exploring in further controlled studies. Persistent symptoms post-concussion are complex, and assessment and treatment of the neck are relatively accessible and affordable.
So like, do it.

Paper? Here ya go!

What do speech therapists do for concussion?

The Gist -This isn’t PT. It’s SLP. But many people with persistent concussion symptoms need SLP care. But what are those SLPs actually doing? Let’s find out.

This piece discusses the role of an SLP in an interdisciplinary team treating persistent post-concussion symptoms. These were all folks at least 6 months after a mild-to-moderate TBI and most where military veterans or retried elite athletes, for context. The SLPs were part of a 6 person interdisciplinary team tasked with taking a holistic approach to improvement. If you’re treating someone with concussion and they haven’t been referred to an SLP, it may be something to look into for them. But first, here’s what an SLP does in this context.

They assess auditory functioning including hypersensitivity to noise and difficulty with auditory processing, something present in 85-89% of people after mTBI (and present in me, too. To this day I have trouble picking out individual voices in loud areas. Please don’t talk to me in the bar, I won’t hear you.). They also assess word finding (something I still have trouble with too), social communication, executive functioning, attention/processing speed, reading difficulty due to visual dysfunction (I have this), verbal memory impairment, aphasia, dysarthria, and other vocal disorders.

So if you’re seeing clues about any of these things in a post-concussive patient, an SLP is the way. But what do they do to treat them? Read on.

Tell Me More -I was going to use the fictitious case reported in this paper to talk about the treatment process. But I’m not going to. Instead, I’m going to use my own experience as the real case, informed by the information in this paper. Upon diagnosis of this last TBI, I was 27 years old and presented with executive functioning deficiencies, word finding problems, acquired stutter, memory struggles, and difficulty managing time. My SLP went through a motivational interviewing process where they helped identify specific barriers in these areas and ways that I could overcome them. I was initially resistant to building out a specific calendar and to do list because I always found them both boring and overwhelming in the past. But the SLP helped me discover that my goals (doing well in school and being less lost and frustrated) needed a plan to be accomplished. Since we weren’t able to come up with a better plan, we decided to test out the strict calendar.

We set goals based on calendar design and adherence and how effective it was at helping me remember assignments to reassess at our next visit in two weeks. They were very helpful at helping me see the positive side of this, that going through this annoying process would make me more present with family and friends, less worried about what I was missing, and more in control of my life. They even embraced my desire to build my own calendar booklet since I’d dabbled in book binding in the past, as long as I could finish it quickly (I did). In the first session, we laid out an hour by hour plan of the next week and also discussed how to react if I wasn’t able to keep it in order to not feel as if I were failing each time I messed up.

That’s very similar to what happened in the fictitious case in this paper. I’d continue, but don’t want to clog too many things up and be discussing this forever. But if you’re interested, do email me I’m happy to discuss my experience in more detail. My time with the SLP helped me feel empowered and in control in a time when it felt like everything was just happening to me. It made my recovery process much smoother and I still use those techniques (on and off) four years later.

That’s a taste of what an SLP can do for your patients, so if they need it, do consider referring to one (or asking their PCP to do so), they can truly be life-changing.

Paper? Got it right here.

That was too wholesome. Let’s talk about depression.

The Gist -This is also something I have (yay!) but we’re not here to talk about me (anymore. Beyond saying that I am being treated and it’s okay and that talking about mental health is important so I’m trying to be more open about it. Thank you for coming to my TED talk). Instead, we’re here to talk about its prevalence in those with persistent post-concussion symptoms. Because boy is it prevalent. This comes from a piece in JAMA Network Open from 2022.

The researchers extracted data from 18 studies including 9101 participants. All were cohort studies and 72% were done with adults. Mean time post concussion was 21 weeks and they pretty much all (and by pretty much, I mean seriously, all) showed an association between depressive symptoms and persistent post-concussion symptoms. I mean, just check out this forest plot.

Tell Me More - Look, we don’t know if depression causes persistent post concussion symptoms or vice versa yet, but we do know that they’re correlated somehow. And we don’t know what the connection could be. In the words of the researchers

While some studies have highlighted the role of the trigeminal cervical complex and pain neuropeptides in specific PPCS, such as headache, depressive symptoms following a concussion could be a direct consequence of postinjury brain changes in the autonomic nervous system, more specifically the amygdala, a brain structure strongly connected to autonomic and neurohormonal axes that plays a crucial role in emotional processing.
But we’re not sure. We’re also not sure if concussion directly causes things or if it causes social isolation that can lead to these things. But we do know they’re correlated, and if your concussion patients aren’t being assessed for this stuff, recommended that they are.
