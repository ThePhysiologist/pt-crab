Issue 174

DNF

I’m hoping the subject of this email isn’t an omen as I’m lining up for a 50K trail race next week. It’s not “Did Not Finish” in this context, rather, we’re talking Deep Neck Flexors. Should you train them? Can you train them? Can you test them? Things like that.

Let’s jump right in.

Does the Craniocervical Flexion Test work?

The Gist -If you’re like me, you’ve probably got one of those light blue Chattanooga pressure biofeedback devices laying around the clinic. I don’t use it much. But maybe I should? This paper has a bit of the answer as it looks into the Craniocervical flexion text to see if it’s worthwhile. If you want the details of the test, see that link. The basics are that you put the unit under the occiput and have your patient gently nod to raise the pressure to certain values and hold it there. If they can do it, pass! If not, fail. The point is to assess the imbalance between the superficial and deep neck flexors. It’s a simple test if you have the tools, but is it reliable, valid, and responsive? This 54 page systematic review from 2020 wanted to find out.

They included 14 studies in their assessment and found some good things overall, but not great. Limited evidence for responsiveness, unknown for measurement error. Good inter and intrarater reliability and convergent validity, but no property had a strong level of evidence. An important part of the whole thing was that the minimum detectable change ranged from 3 to 5mmHg across all studies. This is key because the test is performed in 2mmHg increments, so you can’t see much difference step to step if the MDC is 4.

Tell Me More -Other important things found in the study are that the test does appear to measure what it purports to measure, the activity of the deep neck flexors. This was seen with a moderate level of evidence which is good enough for me. Because most of the studies in this piece were done in a clinical setting, the external validity of them is quite good, another bonus.

There are some downsides in applying to clinical practice though. First, what I mentioned above about MDC and the fact that about half of the studies of the test were done in healthy populations rather than in people with neck pain. The researchers also “recommend caution when using the CCFT as an outcome measure… in clinical practice… because of its low responsiveness.” That is to say that it doesn’t change much or quickly over the course of care.

Overall, this test is “valid and reliable” to use as an assessment tool. Perhaps not an outcome measure, but it can be helpful to see if the muscles are doing what the muscles should do so you know where to start as a clinician.

Paper?Gotcha.

So the test works. What else is there to know?

The Gist -The next step beyond the test, in my mind, is knowing whether training to do better with elements of the test actually helps with neck pain. Once again, we’re using the Chattanooga guy. But if you don’t like him, don’t fret, the next study has exercises that can be done without. The question here is if motor control training with pressure biofeedback can reduce neck pain and disability. And the answer is that it probably can. Which I’ll bet you knew because of positive publication bias anyway.

This was a systematic review published in Musculoskeletal Science and Practice in 2020. They included 17 studies for qualitative synthesis and 10 for quantitative. The top line is that “motor control training of deep neck flexors with pressure biofeedback was found to have equal or better effectiveness on neck pain intensity and disability in comparison to other control interventions.” For more details, let’s look at those control interventions.

10 studies compared the biofeedback to endurance training of cervical muscles, one looked at mobilization, two used proprioceptive training, one used no intervention, and two used active cervical movements. In all the studies, disability was assessed with the NDI and they also looked at pain intensity.

Across the studies, the biofeedback program was better than control in 6, similar in 7, and worse in 1 when it came to controlling pain. For disability, all studies showed that it was effective. 6 showed that it was better than control and 8 that it was about the same.

Overall, it looks good. But there are, as always, more details.

Tell Me More -To dig into those details, let’s look more closely at the comparisons to controls. Biofeedback beat active cervical motion, no treatment, cervical strength-endurance training, and deep neck flexor training without biofeedback. It performed worse than spinal mobilization and equal to cervical proprioceptive training and deep neck extensor training.

What we don’t have are the protocols used in these studies. For that, you’d have to go to each individual one and I don’t want to dive into that right now. My takeaway is that I’ll be investing in a pressure biofeedback unit both for myself and my patients.

Cervicocranial Flexion ain’t better, ain’t worse

The Gist -As you may know by now, I’ve got nonspecific neck pain, and so do many others. These researchers from northern Italy did a systematic review and meta-analysis to see how cervicocranial flexion exercises could make it better. They reviewed 16 articles in the qualitative synthesis and 9 in the quant to find that “moderate to very low-quality evidence that deep cervical muscle exercise protocols are not superior to other types of active exercises in reducing the intensity of pain in people with NSNP.” What this quick synopsis hides is that they’re not worse either.

Eleven of the 16 studies used the same CCF protocol (from this book), 10 had control groups with active exercises, 3 were “wait-and-see” controls, and five had proprioceptive training, passive treatment, or multimodal conventional treatments as their controls. After going through all of them, “The meta-analysis we performed reported no significant overall differences between a CCF protocol and other active exercise protocols.“ Compared to other treatment modalities, it fared worse than cervical and thoracic spine mobilization in one study and equal to proprioceptive training in another 2. Versus no treatment, the CCF protocol was beneficial in two out of the three trials.

Tell Me More -What does this mean for you? The researchers say that the breakthrough here is that the CCF protocol is relatively low load and easy to perform, so being equal to other types of active exercise is actually good news. It’s an option to treat those with non-specific neck pain who need an easier path. None of the studies included if the biofeedback devices were made part of a home program, so it’s possible that CCF could have been even more useful if the patients had been able to take the equipment home.

The most striking bit of all of this research is that the effectiveness mentioned above was based solely on disability measures, pain was not a primary outcome in any trial they looked at. Which is weird, since all these trials were treating non-specific neck pain. It was a secondary outcome measure in some, but you’ll have to check out the paper yourself for those details.

What have we learned today? The Summary

Neck rehab is what you make it. Or, at least, what you want to make it. You can go your own way or you can follow the path we went along here. Have a pressure biofeedback monitor? Use it. Don’t? You don’t need one, but it could work, especially if other things aren’t. What we see overall is that pressure biofeedback is a legit option to decrease neck pain and disability. It could be an especially legit option amongst those dealing with high levels of neck pain, or multiple sources of pain since it’s pretty easy to pull off. Consider learning more about the application and trying to pull if off in clinic some time, you may like it.
