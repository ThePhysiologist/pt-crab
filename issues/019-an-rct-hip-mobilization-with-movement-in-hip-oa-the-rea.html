Issue 019

An RCT Hip Mobilization with Movement in Hip OA, the Real Structure of Vastus Medialis, MWM in Shoulder Impingement, and Ankle Coordination in the Chronically Unstable

This week, we double down on mobilization with movement in both the hip and the shoulder to see what that modality can do for your patients, review a remarkable anatomical study about the structure of the vastus medialis, and look into the details of gait and adaptions in people with chronic ankle instability.

As usual, here’s where I exhort you to tweet, post, etc. this post far and wide to help PT Crab grow. We’re like imperial Rome, we expand or we die, so please help us expand. Thanks!

Let’s dive in.

MWM Decreases Pain and Increases Mobility in People with Hip OA

The Gist -This RCT claims to be the first to look into the immediate effects of mobilization with movement on hip OA and they’re looking pretty positive so far. They randomized 40 patients to MWM or sham treatment, then performed hip flexion MWM and IR MWM to the MWM group. The sham people got a sham version. Immediately afterward, they reassessed pain, range of motion, and a few functional tests.

MWM decreased pain by two points on a 0-10 scale, increased hip flexion range by 12.2°, increased IR by 4.4°, decreased TUG time by 2.7 seconds, and decreased the 40m self-paced walk time by 11.2 seconds compared to sham. Great results overall.

Tell Me More - The paper itself has more details about the specific techniques used, so grab the link below if that’s what you’re into. Here, we’ll talk limitations and why MWM may have helped these people. First, the why.

MWM’s mechanism is not yet known, but there are a bunch of suggestions offered up by the paper: stretch effect on the joint capsules and tissues to restore normal arthrokinematics, altering a joint’s positional faults, changing the pain inhibitory system, or stimulating joint mechanoreceptors. There are others as well. Like much of manual therapy, we don’t really know why yet, we just know that it works.

Speaking of if it works, limitations. This study was pretty small and doesn’t have much corroboration. As I said above, it’s the first of its kind. On the plus side, it was double-blinded in the randomization phase and in data analysis, so that’s a big plus. They only evaluated immediate effects, and only one session of treatment was applied. Lastly, in order to seek specificity, they didn’t combine this with any other treatments, potentially limiting generalizability. Hey, you can’t have it all.

Paper? - It’s here and open access. Enjoy!

What is Vastus Medialis Really Like? Weird.

The Gist -Cadaver studies don’t often feature in PT Crab, since there’s rarely anything clinically applicable, but this interesting study may have some clinical nuggets. They assessed 18 cadaveric lower limbs to study the anatomical interaction between VM and VI and came up with some cool stuff. VM is a clamp muscle. Strange, huh? The figures in the paper really make it clear, so I highly recommend checking those out. I’ll try to explain below.

VM originates from the medial surface of the femur and has most of its fibers directed transversely across the bone, then wrapping around the VI aponeurosis “like a clip holding a sheet of paper.” It uses this clipping action to pull the VI medially while it contracts, changing the direction of force on the patella. Cool! VM also has an insertion on the patella, so it doesn’t only pull VI, but that’s a function of it.

Tell Me More -Let’s talk about why this matters. The researchers point out that this is new information about VM and that it digs deeper into how the muscle controls patellar motion. In their words, it “functions as a dynamic medial stabilizer of the patella”, not via direct pull on the patella via the distal, VMO portion, but mostly through its control of VI’s direction of pull. They also note that this study, combined with previous ones, shows that direct force supplied by VM for knee extension is limited. Specifically:

The EMG observations in the literature, together with findings of the present anatomical study, suggest that the VM acts as an indirect extensor at the knee. It can be hypothesized that by pulling the longitudinal components of VI and rectus femoris medially and dorsally, the VM tightens and shortens these quadriceps muscles, much like a belt around the waist. Obviously, this shortening of the length of the quadriceps (indirect extensor mechanism) is most important during the terminal phase of extension.

This is some interesting new ground in our understanding of knee extension. Check out the full paper for the figures and more details about their new findings.

Here’s That Paper -And it’s open access! Yay!

MWM for Shoulder Impingement, Could it Work Here Too?

The Gist -In a remarkably similar study to the above one about MWM in the hip, this RCT looked into MWM techniques in the shoulder, specifically its immediate effects on shoulder impingement syndrome. They split their 27 patients into two groups and applied 4 sessions of MWM to one while the other got 4 sessions of sham, then switched the interventions and collected data all along the way.

The MWM used was a posterior and inferior glide of the the humeral head as the patient flexed the shoulder in a seated position. The results? Well, the paper drowns us in data, but none of it is very good for the intervention. DASH changed by less than 1 point, well below MCID, and all range of motion values changed by minuscule amounts, most less than 1 degree. It looks like this one was a no-go. The only positive change was SPADI pain, which changed by a clinically important amount, but it wasn’t a primary outcome measure and doesn’t rescue this treatment as useful.

Tell Me More -It’s rare that negative results get featured anywhere, but they’re an important part of science and deserve to be displayed here at PT Crab. We don’t really have much to add in the limitations or expansion area, since there’s not a ton of interesting stuff that comes out of a study that doesn’t show an effect. It does demonstrate that a theoretically useful treatment like MWM needs to be assessed in each motion individually before we can say that it’s useful everywhere, since it seems to be showing some inconsistencies.

Paper?Sure thing.

What’s the Difference Between People with Chronic Ankle Instability and Those Without?

The Gist -This incredibly detailed study looked into the coordination and coping strategies of people with chronic ankle instability (CAI) to give us some things to watch out for when treating them. They had 17 people in each group (CAI, those who coped with CAI, and control) run on a treadmill at 2.68 meters per second (yea, idk what that means either so I checked. It’s 6mph) and recorded kinematic data to detect differences between the groups.

Here are the bits we can use:
All three groups have unique coordination strategies, people with ankle instability lead with their tibias when transitioning from loading to propulsion, while controls lead via plantar flexion. This could contribute to the reduced power at the ankle joint seen in people with CAI.

People with CAI have less variable gaits, potentially exposing them to more injury risk due to more focused load on the same points over and over.

Tell Me More -In some ways, this study is a huge information dump that doesn’t produce a lot of clinical applicability, but rather shows some potential warnings and areas of focus for clinicians treating people with CAI. Specifically, they’re at higher risk for future injury due to limited available strategies to overcome high rates of loading, so loading their ankles in new, unexpected ways could help lower this risk.

I’m not going to parse through all the details of the rest, as it’s quite complicated. If you like gait kinematics and data though, it’s a bit of a gold mine and I highly recommend giving it a look.

Where’s that paper then? Right here. Though, unfortunately, not open access.
