The Vitality of Vitals in the Leg

The Gist -Coming from the aforementioned JOSPT Cases, this article presents an interesting diagnosis arrived at by the focus and quick response of the PT. A 28-year-old woman referred to PT by her PCP was complaining of bilateral posterolateral hip pain that hadn’t showed any problems on X-ray. It got worse while walking and climbing stairs and she didn’t have any history of trauma. After palpation, a bunch of special tests, and eval, she was diagnosed with piriformis syndrome and was given stretching to do and advised to use heat to calm it down. 5 days later with no results, the PT did some claudication tests and found big issues in BP. “The dorsalis pedis, femoral, and popliteal artery pulses were absent bilaterally. Blood pressure in the right and left arms were 106/68 mmHg and 100/76 mmHg, respectively, but 83/56 mmHg on the left lower limb and not recordable on the right lower limb.” O.o

After referring out for imaging, scans showed she had narrowing of the left renal artery and thickening in the abdominal aorta in a condition called Takayasu arteritis, AKA Pulseless Disease. Take those vitals kids.

Tell Me More -This uncommon vasculitis is a chronic inflammatory disorder that’s the most common vasculitis in Japan. Most common in women under 40 and of Asian descent, Takayasu’s arthritis usually demonstrates a systemic phase and an occlusive phase. This particular patient was deep into the occlusive phase and presented with rare symptoms, as hip pain has not previously been associated with the condition. Calf pain is common, as is orthostatic hypotension and headaches. A few months after diagnosis, the patient had surgery that completely eliminated her hip pain and returned her to full activity. The patient had reported that other practitioners hadn’t taken her pain seriously, so kudos PT! Well done.

VOMS in Concussion High School v. College

The Gist -Researchers publishing in the Journal of Athletic Training wanted to know if there were differences in how high school and collegiate athletes presented on the Vestibular/Ocular Motor Screening Assessment post-concussion and beyond. Turns out, there weren’t differences. But there is more to learn here.

The main finding of the study was negative, which is good! We need negative results to be published. The secondary findings are potentially interesting. These were all high school or collegiate athletes (110 of them) who were initially assessed within 72 hours of a sport-related concussion. They all presented similarly on the VOMS total, VOMS change, and convergence distance tests in the acute phase, leading to the negative result. The interesting new information is that these athletes consistently showed scores that were higher than the “probable concussion” cutoff scores even though they were diagnosed with concussions via other measures. So if the cutoff scores don’t work to show how these athletes are recovering from concussions, what does? The researchers recommend watching the change scores and not letting the cutoffs affect clinical reasoning in concussion recovery.

Tell Me More -”Given that the primary goal of the multifaceted assessment is to diagnose SRC, cutoff scores for the VOMS components were established using total scores to aid in identifying athletes with concussion.10 The focus on total scores to simply identify injured individuals may narrow clinicians’ interpretation of the VOMS, leaving them susceptible to missing clinically relevant information.” That’s what these researchers say about the cutoffs, and it’s borne out by the data: “The symptom provocation of approximately 60% of participants was greater than the cutoff score for horizontal VOR, vertical VOR, and VMS. Conversely, 73.64% to 85.45% of participants did not experience symptom provocation great enough to exceed the cutoff scores for smooth pursuits and NPC distance.” Obviously, this one study doesn’t throw out the VOMS cutoffs, it just challenges them and the note above from the researchers shows how to use them cautiously. Always keep your clinical reasoning alight.

Scap ștab for medial epicondylab? Maybe.

The Gist - Another case report here, following up on research that has shown how scapular stabilization exercises work in lateral epicondylalgia, these PTs tried out similar exercises for medial epicondylalgia with pretty good results. They started down the path because “manual scapular correction into posterior tip relieved the pain experienced with resisted elbow flexion.” Other than the arm pain that interfered with ADLs, the patient (a 52-year-old woman) had 5/5 strength throughout and all ROM was within normal limits. Based on the data acquired, the PTs targeted exercises at her middle and lower traps as well as her rhomboids, working on strength, endurance, and scapular motor control.

Once a week of PT for 8 weeks with a daily HEP that was advanced throughout was the plan and she looked good. They used the QuickDASH as the primary outcome measure and the patient met the MCID (3/11 change) for pain and the QuickDASH as well (changing by 8 points) at the one month follow up.

Tell Me More -So where are we now? That previous lateral epicondylalgia paper opened the door to this treatment technique but it seems like the manual scapular correction is what caused the PTs to step through it. What this paper really shows is the value of clinical reasoning with some data to back it up. We don’t yet know if this is a generally valid technique for treating epicondylalgia, but give this case report (and the lateral one above) a read to make the decision on your own, the authors have some good thoughts in the discussion section.

If you PASS the PASS, you can go home.

The Gist -This study assessed the Postural Assessment Scale for Stroke (PASS) and its ability to determine acute care discharge recommendations. It was run on the neurology floor of a 782 bed (how specific!) acute care hospital with 153 participants. The PASS is a newish outcome measure for stroke that hasn’t been used consistently yet but is highly recommended by APTA StrokEDGE and this research team wanted to see if it could be helpful and quick in deciding discharge plans.

They taught their PTs how to pull it off, sent them into the wards, then collected data to see how the PASS fit with discharge recommendations. They didn’t use PASS to discharge, they just wanted to see how it lined up with their PT’s judgments. It was used within a median of 48 hours of admission and seemed to line up very well with discharge recomendations. Patients with a mean score of 31.8 (±2) were sent home with home PT, a 32.6 (±3) sent home with outpatient PT recomendations, 34.9 (±1) without PT recomendations, 15.7 (±8) to acute care rehab, and 9.5 (±11) to a SNF. This linear relationship and its ease of administration make it a good test to add to the battery of items in determining discharge recomendations.

Tell Me More - The PASS is a 12-item scale with each activity scored in a 0-3 scale. Activities include standing without support, sitting without support, standing and picking up a pencil from the floor, etc. It’s all balance based and a full breakdown is on the SRAlab site here.  There’s also a well-validated short form with just 5 items that the paper indicates could be useful as well. Overall, it assesses mobility and balance and has less of a floor and ceiling effect than the Berg Balance Scale, potentially making it more sensitive when used with severely and mildly impaired persons.

The authors are very specific to recommend it as a tool to guide discharge, but it does not predict performance on ADLs, so it can’t be used as the whole testing battery before sending the patient home. It’s strictly posture and balance. This paper also doesn’t indicate cutoff scores for discharge recommendations, that’s not what it was for. Rather, it shows that the PASS scales linearly with other indicators of discharge recommendations and asks for future studies to assess cutoffs. Keep an eye out for those.
