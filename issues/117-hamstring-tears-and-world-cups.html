Hamstring Tears and World Cups

In honor of the World Cup Final Final on Sunday (because technically this tournament is called the World Cup Final since the rest is World Cup Qualifying) and the 3rd place game on Saturday (featuring Morocco and Croatia), we’re talking hamstring strains and tears today. Because they’re all too common in soccer/football. One of the papers is even an amazingly in-depth case report on a player from the Premier League recovering from a hamstring tear. It’s one of the most complete protocol breakdowns I’ve ever seen.

We’re also looking at a piece from the Journal of Athletic Training on hamstring injury rehabilitation and another on whether pain-free should be the threshold to work from in rehab. And I’m also reminding you about the CPG on the topic that JOSPT put out earlier this year. You can find that and all other known CPGs here in this massive list you’ve helped me put together. My readers help me find new and updated ones via the form on that page. If you have more to contribute, please let me know!

How to Heal a Hamstring

The Gist -This piece from the JAT (a fantastic journal, btw) presents a straightforward approach to hamstring injury rehab while breaking down some complexity too. They point out that “In the second half of the swing, the hamstring are active, rapidly lengthening, and absorbing energy to decelerate the limb in preparation for foot contact” and that “Hamstring muscle force increases approximately 1.3-fold as running velocity increases from 80% to 100% of maximum and the greatest muscle-tendon unit stretch is incurred by the long head of the biceps femoris.” As folks who know about muscles, you know that it’s dangerous to put out more and more force as a muscle reaches its maximal length. No wonder so many people strain hamstrings.

For diagnosis, they cite research that points out that previous hamstring injury “increases the risk of future… by 2.7 times”. Usually palpation, ROM, and strength testing combine to diagnose the injury. Maximal pain provocation lets one zoom in on the exact location and ROM can provide good indication of tear severity. Lastly, pain rating during isometric contraction and strength of contraction indicate severity as well. In the soccer player paper for King Crab supporters, they use a scale on a box to test strength. The player puts their leg in 90-90 position then pushes down on the scale, forcefully extending their hip and flexing their knee and the maximum value the scale reads indicates current strength. Pretty simple and cool!

Lastly on dx, MRIs haven’t been shown to be too helpful in guiding rehab. In fact, diagnostic ultrasound could be even better, but we don’t have data that shows either as too helpful.

Tell Me More -Now for the rehab. They recommend a 3 stage process for return to running, starting when the person can walk with pain less than 4 on a 0-10 scale. This is gradual progression from slow jogging to moderate running. Then slow progression into faster and faster running with great care taken with exposure to sprinting, only increasing speed 5% at a time as you approach maximal running velocity. For the exercises that help that rehab, they recommend eccentric loading even early in the process. As soon as someone can get through full ROM with pain \<4/10, they recommend a combination of eccentric knee flexion, long hip extension, and short hip extension exercises. I included a figure to better define what they’re talking about.

They also have a series of videos showing the specific exercises recommended. Here are links to those:
- Submaximal slider exercises: http://doi.org/10.4085/1062-6050-0707.20.S1
- Hip extensor exercises: http://doi.org/10.4085/1062-6050-0707.20.S2
- Hip thrusts: http://doi.org/10.4085/1062-6050-0707.20.S3

They also recommend progressive agility and trunk exercises that can help control lumbopelvic position and reduce hamstring length in running. Alongside all that, there recommend hamstring flexibility exercises “if deficits persist during rehabilitation, as greater deficits in active knee-extension ROM at RTS have been associated with an increased risk of subsequent hamstring injury” and data shows that “Recovery of active knee-extension ROM can be slightly accelerated by implementing passive hamstring stretching 4 times per day, compared with once daily, starting at 48 hours after hamstring injury.”

Lastly, there’s limited evidence that running technique drills and manual therapy are helpful in most people, jsyk.

I’m out of space except to say that they recommend the Askling H-test to test for pain and apprehension and eccentric strength testing to prep for return to sport.

There’s a lot more in the paper, but I think you’ll agree that’s a lot already. If you want more, read it!

How Much Is Too Much? Pain and Hamstring Tears

The Gist - These investigators wanted to see if there’s a better way to rehab hamstrings by working up to a pain threshold rather than staying in pain-free areas. They point out that the CPGs allow this, but no one has yet tested it. So they recruited 44 people with hamstring strains and put them through the protocols.

Both groups performed standardized rehab 2x/wk with hamstring strengthening exercises and progressive running. During each exercise they were asked to rate pain on a 0-10 scale. One group was stopped if pain was ever >0 while the other was only stopped if pain was >4/10. Their strength exercises were largely eccentric and included bridges, hip extension, eccentric sliders, and the Nordic hamstring exercise. The running protocol was a 9 stage protocol devised by Silder et al. in this 2013 paper. Outcome measures were time to return to play, biceps femoris long head fascicle length, isometric knee flexor strength, fear of movement, and number of reinjuries in following 6 months.

In brief, the 4/10 group returned to play in a median time of 17 days while the pain-free group took a median of 15, data that turned out not to be statistically significant. The pain threshold group had significantly more improved biceps femoris fascicle length, knee isometric contraction strength, and fear of movement compared to the pain-free group.

Tell Me More - In less brief, things were a bit mixed but basically equal between the two groups. The 4/10 group was a bit better than 0/10 on some, but a bit worse on others. Both groups had 2 reinsures in the 6 months after rehabilitation (sad :( ).

In a moderately brief close here, let’s talk fascicle length because I hadn’t heard about it as an outcome measure before. Fascicle length is a modifiable risk factor for hamstring injury that was chosen as a criteria because some previous writers “hypothesized that a lack of eccentric loading and long-length exercise during early rehabilitation may contribute to residual deficits and the elevated risk of reinjury seen in previously injured hamstring.” As far as actually measuring it, they used ultrasound and methods from this study.

How to Rehab a Premier League Soccer Player

The Gist -If you don’t already know, the Premier League is the top soccer league in the UK and arguably the top one in Europe, making it tops in the entire world (which is my opinion). And if you don’t support a particular team in it, choose Liverpool. Just do it. Liverpool. Liverpool. Liverpool.

This paper comes from their rival team that play just across the river, literally a very good stone’s throw away from them on the other side of the Mersey. It’s an incredibly detailed look into the return to play process of a defender who strained their hamstring. They don’t give the name of the player, but give an incredible amount of detail about their playing career leading this writer to think that they’re most likely Muhamed Bešić. They hurt they’re hamstring about 9 minutes into a match after over-stretching it for an errant pass. Post-match he had a positive SLR of 85° and altered sensation, but no pain at rest nor with gait. An MRI “revealed a longitudinal partial-thickness split tear of the intramuscular portion of the conjoint tendon, demonstrating a long craniocaudal length tear involving the tendinous and aponeurotic portions.”

They began with a POLICE protocol. Protection, optimal loading, ice, compression, elevation. With daily loading of submax isometrics to maximal isometrics initially and the previously mentioned leg on a scale test (AKA Isometric posterior chain test) 6 days in to quantify maximal force production and progress through rehab.

Tell Me More -If you’re in sports PT, seriously, read this thing. It’s legit. What I’m going to talk about is the control-chaos continuum, CCC, they put the player through. They slowly exposed him to more and more risky activities in training and secretly created conditions similar to his injury condition into the protocol to ensure he didn’t continue to have fear of that movement. Transition into soccer-specific work was done on day 19, the beginning of the chaos phase as the paper puts it.

They were able to use their ongoing scale testing (AKA IPC) to verify that increasingly intense training sessions weren’t mucking up injury recovery by taking this data every day and reading it alongside data from player movement on the pitch. Overall, the player returned to preinjury running load output 35 days post injury and played again 40 days post injury. He did not suffer a reinjury that season and if it was actually Muhamed Bešić and Wikipedia is correct has not suffered reinjury to date. He even had a match at 1.26x standard game load about three weeks after returning to play and was fine after it. Yay!

I can’t go into any more detail here but seriously if you like sports rehab you’ll love this. There’s detail and data galore and it’s a real peek into what is going on at top soccer clubs and this was 7 years ago! Who knows that they’re doing now?! Seriously, check it out.
