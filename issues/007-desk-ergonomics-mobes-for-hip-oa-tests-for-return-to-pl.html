Desk Ergonomics, Mobes for Hip OA, Tests for Return to Play, and External Ventricular Drains

This week’s PT Crab features our first acute care article, as well as one that dives into ergonomics, another on hip osteoarthritis, and still another about when and how often to test athletes returning to sport after ACL reconstruction. Some studies are light, some are dense, all are interesting and hopefully give you some new treatment approaches or ideas to help a myriad of patients.

Rise up! Sit to Stand Workstations for Pain Prevention? Maybe…

The Gist -Many people have moved their offices home over the last few months and it’s been just long enough for poor ergonomics to catch up to them. Fortunately, this systematic review may have some guidance in how to adapt their workstations to improve LBP and neck pain. Unfortunately, there weren’t too many studies to pull from, but the authors were still able to gather a bit of data.

Three studies showed improved neck, lower back, and upper back pain with moderate clinical effect sizes when participants switched from traditional to standing desks. Another found that participants experienced more upper back and neck pain with a standing desk, but less low back pain. In general, the studies showed a positive effect of transitioning from sit to stand throughout the day.

How can this help your patients? There are a few on-desk attachments that allow the transition from sit to stand (like the Varidesk) and a few desks that adjust in height (including cheapish ones from Ikea) that could be recommended. Some people have been known to put chairs or cardboard boxes on their current desks to lift them up higher, but it’s important to ensure that their keyboards stay low, otherwise they’re at risk for upper trap and levator scap pain due to prolonged shrugging to type.

Tell Me More - This systematic review popped up in the journal Physical Therapy Reviews last year, but didn’t have much to pull from as they dug through the research. There were no high quality (by PEDro score) studies to include and only 15 overall. There just hasn’t been much research into sit-to-stand workstations, at least as the review authors defined them. Many results were clouded by other factors as well: one study only pulled from overweight and obese populations and another used workers at a call-center who habitually cradle phones between their ears and shoulders, possibly leading to neck pain that the study captured. The overall, cloudy conclusion from the research is put best by the authors:

Overall, findings suggest SSW may not absolutely relieve chronic neck or LBP but should not be excluded from pain management recommendations due to potential of positive impact.
That’s all we can definitively say on the matter today.

Mobilizations for Hip OA? Do it! Do it!

The Gist - This study comes by way of Poland and the Journal of Back and Musculoskeletal Rehabilitation. The researchers dug into the effects fo mobilizations on hip OA and the results were exceedingly positive. They entered 57 women into two groups, one that received NWB exercises and the other hip mobes. Specifically, they received two hip tractions (both in supine, one pulling laterally and one pulling down the leg) and one mobe (ER and Abduction) three times per week for two weeks for a total of 20 minutes per session. The NWB group performed mobility exercises for the same amount of time. Both groups also received therapeutic ultrasound and magnetic therapy (just because I guess, they don’t really say).

What happened? Good things! The researchers used the Lequesne index of severity of OA and the LEFS as their main outcome measures, as well as hip ROM and pain. The mobilization group saw significant improvements in pain, Lequesne index, and LEFS, and motion improvements in extension, abduction, and internal rotation when compared to the NWB control. They got better all across the board.

Tell Me More -This is one of the less clouded studies that we see at PT Crab. It’s straightforward and suffers from fewer flaws than many. The selection criteria was a bit limited, as the researchers only took women aged 55-65, so that’s a note to watch out for. Otherwise, the intervention was simple as was the control group.

In their discussion section, the authors point to a few studies that showed opposite results to their own. Notably, most research they pull used single bouts of manual therapy, rather than multiple as in this study. This study indicates that multiple sessions is probably the key to manual therapy’s effectiveness and that one or two bouts won’t be enough to demonstrate any changes.

When to Test the ACL for Return to Play. It’s Less Often than You’d Think.

The Gist -This collection of ATCs and MDs from the University of Virginia did a deep dive into athlete assessment for ACL reconstruction as they tried to figure out the ideal time to test for return to play in their participants. They recruited 293 patients into their case-control study, with 40 completing it through to the end. They split participants into groups based on how long it had been since their ACL surgery, then assessed knee extensor and flexor torque and symmetry and the International Knee Documentation Committee subjective form.

The details of the statistical breakdown are complex (and worth reading, if you’re into that), but this is the gist, so the gist is: two months between return to play assessments is needed to show significant gains. In their patient sample, it required that much time to make clinically significant improvements. Why wait two whole months? Frustration. The authors note that both clinicians and patients get frustrated with the marginal gains seen late in the rehabilitation process, as ACL strength gains trickle in after 6 months post surgery. Stretching out the return to play testing time helps avoid frustration and achieve clinically significant changes in strength and symmetry, helping prevent over-testing and increased frustration about slow gains in strength.

Tell Me More - At every increasing time point post-surgery, patients were stronger and stronger. That’s not surprising, but where the gains are made is. Researchers found that there aren’t significant differences between assessments at 7-8 months and 8-9 months, nor do they exist between 6-7 and 7-8. There is a significant difference between those assessed between months 5-6 and 6-7. Based on their data, it seems that marginal, slower gains come beyond month 6, which is why slowing down return to play assessments is a good call.

Though it sheds light on an interesting area, this study is a slightly weird one that has its share of limitations. There was no patient randomization and they self-selected when to be referred to return-to-sport assessment. The type of rehab that patients went through also wasn’t controlled. It’s possible that an ideal rehab protocol could speed up strength and symmetry gains and close down the 2 month recommendation to 6 weeks or less, but we just don’t know yet, at least based on this research. As I said above, there are some great statistical breakdowns in the paper that dig into the details on how predictive the return-to-sport assessment is with each value involved, so I highly recommend reading it if that’s the kind of stuff you like.

Alright, gimme the paper. Yupp.

Mobes with Ventricular Drains? Can you do it? Yea, Probably.

The Gist -The first acute care paper of PT Crab features a retrospective review from the Journal of Acute Care Physical Therapy about mobilizing patients with external ventricular drains. The researchers dug into data to figure out how many therapists it takes to safely mobilize someone with an external ventricular drain and how experienced those therapists should be. Early and regular mobilization is required for good outcomes in the ICU, so the authors wanted to see the minimum number of therapists required to move a patient safely in order to make this an easier process for hospital staff to pull off.

The overall news was good and interesting. The researchers found that across the 185 encounters with 90 patients, 81% tolerated mobilization out of bed and 53% were able to walk with assistance or better. Only 4 adverse events were recorded across the study, none of which resulted in significant patient harm. 38% of encounters required just one therapist, 57% required two, and only 5% required three to be present. In general, less experienced therapists (\<3yr) performed more out of bed activities than more experienced ones (>10yr), though this could be due to self-selection bias for harder cases by more experienced therapists. Based on this data, it seems that complex patients can be safely mobilized by less experienced practitioners, possibly easing staff resource management in neuro-ICUs.

Tell Me More - This study comes with one caveat that’s quite specific to the hospital in which it was performed (Thomas Jefferson University Hospital). They have a long on-boarding process before therapists work with patients with EVDs, where new therapists are trained by experienced ones, then mentored until they can consistently demonstrate their ability to work with EVDs and understand their complexity in regard to physical therapy. Hospitals without this training process may see different results and needs in these populations. In fact, a similar study of Canadian physical therapists who did not have this mentoring showed that the less experienced therapists were much less likely to perform out of bed mobilizations with their patients, lending credence to this idea.
