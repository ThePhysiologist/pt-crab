Issue 181

Dealing with Dementia

If you’ve been working for more than a few months, you’ve worked with people who have dementia. It’s a challenge, but I’m here to help make it easier. Well, me and these two excellent papers on the topic. We’ll be looking into how interventions based on physical activity work, first, then diving into recommendations on how to do a better job treating people who have dementia. The second paper is a real doozy, almost a how to guide to the topic. I loved reading it and I think you’ll enjoy learning from it. I’ve already used some of the skills I learned from it in the hospital, this very morning.

Dementia affects more than 50 million people worldwide and that number is expected to triple by 2050. Cognitive decline is a hallmark of the condition, but physical decline usually occurs as well. There’s conflicting evidence of what PT can do to help, but the systematic review here gets into it. Let’s check it out.

Exercise and Dementia

The Gist -They looked into 16 studies in 13 countries. 7 used aerobic training or aerobic plus strength training, 5 used multimodal physical activity, and 4 used physical activity plus cognitive training. Cognitive function was the primary outcome measure in 14 of these studies, so they went right at it.

Continuing on the good side, exercise intensity was moderate to high and duration ranged from 9 weeks to 15 months. The Borg scale or HR measurements were used to check on intensity. Usually, exercise isn’t nearly intensive enough, so I’m glad they stepped things up here.

Let’s see what happened:
- On cognitive decline
- Aerobic or aerobic plus strength interventions were mixed. 3 showed positive effects of activity on slowing cognitive decline while one large one showed it may have worsened it.
- Physical activity plus cognitive training had more unequivocal results. The combination of activity and cognitive training seemed to do better than cognitive or physical training alone.
- Overall, there’s insufficient evidence to show that physical activity improves or slows cognitive decline.
- On ADLs
- Physical activity of most modes improved performance on ADLs
- This includes ADL-specific training, cycling, or aerobic walking
- On physical function and mobility
- Physical activity improved mobility, walking distance and endurance, strength, balance, grip strength, muscle strength, and physical fitness
- On QoL
- Unknown. 3 studies assessed this and the results were mixed.
- On behavioral and psychological symptoms
- Three studies assessed this and physical activity seemed to help. They assessed depressive symptoms, behavioral issues, and the psychological symptoms associated with dementia.

Tell Me More -There’s not a whole lot more to tell here, they were to the point. The tools the studies used to assess cognitive decline were solid, as were the interventions, but there haven’t been enough unequivocal answers to give us unequivocal results. Generally, changes were small and may not have been big enough to be clinically significant.

Rehabilitating people who live with dementia

The Gist -Even if physical activity doesn’t help with cognitive decline, we need to know how to effectively rehabilitate people who have dementia. They’re in our hospitals, clinics, and rehab facilities. You may have treated someone with dementia today and not even known it. The early stages aren’t always clear. But once someone enters cognitive impairment, you can’t treat people living with dementia the same as everyone else, it takes more specific work. This paper offers a model based on a narrative literature review with the model broken into 4 categories:
- Relationship: establish personal connection
- Communication: use intentional verbal and nonverbal skills
- Motor learning: understand and exploit strength
- Environment: create safe, comfortable space

Let’s go into each of those (I don’t have space for all (read the paper!), but want to get to a fair bit).

Personal connection

If you have the time to learn them, knowing the names of important people, place, and things can be very helpful. Often, “integrating the name of a loved one or a specific hobby in the context of goal setting can be heartening to a confused patient.” But sometimes this can lead to paranoia, so be aware.

They also recommend you “recognize and accept the offered reality.“ In their words, “perhaps PTs should consider it a privilege to enter the offered reality and do so with grace and humility, rather than feel compelled to force someone back into a world that no longer comforts them.”

Lastly in this category, they recommend you learn what brings joy and triggers negativity. Understanding what is a good life to a person is needed in all patient interactions and can be especially so here. Find out what success means to a patient and celebrate it as it is achieved.

Purposeful Communication

“Empirical research directing best practice is scarce, and very little is known about how [individuals living with dementia] experience communication.” It is entangled with relationship building, as each individual is going to experience your communication differently. “Person-centered care requires a mindset of ‘working with’ rather than ‘doing to’.”

In that vein, verbal communication should be kept simple and clear. Using yes or no questions can enhance communication, as can repetition, paraphrasing, and patience. But do not use “elder-speak”, which can lead to “decreased self-esteem and resistiveness to care.” Pace purposefully and allow time for information processing

Non-verbally, stay measured and relaxed and make regular eye contact. People with dementia may be more sensitive to non-verbal communication than verbal, so pat attention to the messages your face and body language are sending.

Often, for someone with dementia, their behavior is communication. “A patient who removes her shirt in the PT gym feels hot! Uncertainty, discomfort, or fear can lead to cantankerous behavior.” What people do is intended to tell you something that their words may not be able to do.

When faced with unexpected behaviors, PTs must problem solve! Pain or fear may masquerade as agitation; fatigue or confusion may cause disengagement. Before concluding a patient is “uncooperative” or “non-participatory,” the PT should work to get to the source of obstructive behaviors and try to manage the root problem.

Be flexible and adapt to your patients’ needs. This includes cuing strategies, validation of feelings, and finding the communication pattern that works for your patient. People living with dementia may vary in these areas more than most people, so it’s up to you to modify your approach to assist them.

Motor learning

People with dementia learn differently. Late stage dementia makes it very difficult to learn, if possible at all, but early stage can present challenges too. To assist, there are some intentional steps to take.

Prioritize procedural learning. That is, learning by doing with repetitive practice and limited cognitive oversight. In other words, just do things. Other strategies can be effective, but just doing things is the priority that research has directed us toward. Make sure these things are relevant to the person you’re working with and set functional goals the fit with them in order to pull this off effectively.

Make practice sessions repetitive, consistent, constant, and blocked. Motor learning in most people “favors variable, random practice sessions, which aim to broaden motor programs, preparing learned for real world, unpredictable demands.” But that doesn’t work so well in people with dementia since it requires them to, “1] store and later retrieve performance data, 2] evaluate performance on different versions of tasks, and 3] move easily between tasks. Instead, you should use blocked sessions of one task after another, ensuring that the tasks are functional, relevant, and specific, in order to best facilitate learning.

Errorless learning is another method that can be effective. Don’t allow guessing, take a step-wise approach, model the behavior, and slowly space out the retrieval time. Errorless learning has been research-demonstrated to be superior to the errorful, trial and error methods most of us take. It is difficult to perform errorless training, but helpful in these cases.

Lastly in this area, and a theme we often see in training, make it intense and challenging. People with dementia can work hard. Give them opportunities to do so.

Environment

Make it as safe, calm, predictable, and comfortable as you can. It’s not always possible for you to do so, but try your best. This is useful with any patients, but especially those living with dementia. You can’t connect with a patient if they’re distracted.

Be consistent and keep it familiar. Routines are reassuring and they add familiarity beyond even memory. Consistency in place, people, and timing can enhance success. That could mean having the same tech greet the patient each time, use the same table or room, and/or have the appointments at the same time of day. All of these things can help nudge the appointment to be a little more successful.

Final thoughts

Some key points come in the conclusion

Dementia brings the slow demise of memory, function, and identity. In reorienting the focus from what is lost to what abilities remain and potential for gain, PTs create opportunity for rehabilitation success. This framework is intended to help PTs understand some idiosyncrasies of ILwD and exploit strengths and positive characteristics. A “strength-based approach” in dementia care has shown some success and is pivotal to person-centered care.

The authors point out that this is a general model that is designed to be flexible. It’s a starting point for you to build on, adapt, and improve in your own practice.

Take elements, try them out, see which work and which don’t for you, and keep experimenting to design the best possible model.
