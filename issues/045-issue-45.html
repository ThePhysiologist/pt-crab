Just Get A Goni

The Gist -Look, you can’t estimate shoulder posture just by looking at it. I know you think you can, but these guys proved that you can’t. These researchers grabbed a bunch of PT students and showed them 30 images of a bunch of different shoulder postures, then asked them to estimate abduction, flexion, ER, IR, or extension values. They missed, badly. All flexion, extension, and IR/ER estimates were significantly different from measured values, while they only significantly missed on abduction when the value was greater than 90°. They missed flexion by an average of 11°, extension by an average of 8°, IR by about 11° and ER by around 10°. Not good. And I know, I know, you’re not a PT student so you can do a lot better. And maybe you can, but if you’ve been estimating postures for years, maybe reconsider your process, use a goni for a few weeks, and reset your perspective.

Tell Me More -Previous literature cited by the study has shown that PTs are bad at estimating knee, ankle, and neck motion as well, so this miss is nothing new. A different study also showed that experienced PTs could estimate one particular shoulder flexion posture within about 3°, so that’s good, but we don’t have enough data yet to see if that holds for anything else. Reliability between examiners for that study was just fair, and other data has shown that healthy shoulders (the type used in this study and most of these studies) are easier to assess than pathologic ones. In one study, the standard error of measurement of a pathological shoulder was 13.8°. Does it feel like I’m working really hard to convince you not to estimate when you need an actual value? Good. Because this study and others have shown that you probably can’t do it. So stop doing it.

Hip Arthroscopy Sucks. And Patients May Not Expect That.

The Gist -I’m not saying that the surgery is bad, just that it’s tough because it’s like, a surgery. When people be cuttin’ you, it’s going to take time to recover. Unfortunately, the results of this qualitative study show that patients may not expect that and there’s a significant mismatch between their expectations and realities. 17 Australian patients, mean age 36, were interviewed for this study. Their surgeries were all arthroscopic, and “included osteoplasty, acetabular rim resection, ligamentum tires repair, labral repair, and microfracture.” The researchers had a few takeaways, but the main one was teh big difference between expectations and reality. Here are some quotes from their interviews:

I’ve had such a mixed view from several different people, physio’s , osteo’s and obviously the surgeon – it would be anything from a 3 to a 12 month recovery and other people had told me it’ll be 2 years before you are back to yourself (Patient 5).
It was round the 12-week mark where you could be running I believe, or pain-free running or something...and I was just nowhere near there. I feel I’m not there, but, I think at that point was when I started to doubt that it had worked for me and I got really upset about it (Patient 11).
(It’s) this last 3 months that my frustration has more grown, because it hasn’t progressed for me in the way that I would have thought. That’s been really hard (Patient 14).
There are a lot more great quotes in the paper too, so I highly recommend you check it out. Mainly, the paper argues that people need more clear ideas of how rehab will work, more consistency between providers about what to expect, and more discussion of the emotional toll this rehab can take on them.

Tell Me More -I’d love to! Mostly, I’ll do it in quotes again. If you’re engaged enough to get here, I highly recommend you read the whole paper, it’s an easy-ish read and provides a great window into what these patients are thinking.

I trusted every single person I went to see and I think I have learnt, pessimistic as it sounds, to do your own research as well, because I was told so many mixed things, and was so convincingly told, that it was something and it was incredibly upsetting (Patient 11) .
So I lost faith in all the physios and osteos really, so I’m kind of just branching out on my own with my own research that I’ve done. One of the physios that I go to, she sort of contradicted what the surgeon was saying because he said absolutely no like clams, or bridges, or lunges until a certain point...and my physio actually wanted me to do that (Patient 2).

That’s the last of the patient quotes, and I’ll end with the researchers’ conclusions where they speculate on where we can go from here.

Four key themes emerged from the study: 1) a mismatch between expectations and actual physical activity; 2) wide variations in physical activity levels; 3) suboptimal psychological readiness and associated emotional toll; and 4) the influence of available support and information. These findings suggest there are clear opportunities for optimizing patient education and support while addressing relevant barriers and facilitators around return to physical activity.

Your Smartphone Can Analyze Running Kinematics! Kind of.

The Gist - Researchers compared a super fancy 3D kinematics rig to a simple, smartphone app to show that yea, it basically works to analyze running gait. With some limitations. They were able to accurately gauge rear foot e/inversion at touchdown and hip, ankle, and rear foot in/eversion at toe-off. It was poor at showing hip kinematics at touchdown and knee at toe-off, so watch out for that. Overall though, great news.

Here’s our caveat. This only works if you have the smartphone held steadily at the correct height (like on a tripod) and if you use a stylus to trace the angles rather than your finger. Oh! The app is called Coach’s Eye. And isn’t free, jsyk. Available for Apple and Android devices.

Tell Me More -They sampled 10 strides from 18 different, healthy female recreational runners who were covered in reflective markers. Inter-rater reliability for all the measures was excellent, ranging from .95-.99, while MDCs ranged from 1.9-4.4%. That’s pretty darn good. Of the more accurate measures indicated above, none had more than a 2° difference between the fancy 3D camera rig and the smartphone app. That’s great news for cheap gait analysis! Lastly, to make things a bit more expensive, the researchers recommend you don’t use a smartphone camera. Rather, they say you should take the video with a cam that has a higher frame rate (>60Hz) then import it if you want to be as accurate as they are. Something like a GoPro can pull that off and they’re pretty cheap, so it may be a good investment if this is what you want to do.

Unsurprisingly, Identifying as Trans Makes Healthcare Tougher

The Gist -This paper comes from the world of athletic training and deals with it exclusively, but similar research hasn’t been done in PT yet and this is a very important issue so I thought I’d grab it for you. As of 2016, about 0.6% of US adults identify as transgender. It doesn’t sound like a lot, but that’s 1.4 million people. Researchers sent out a survey and eventually received responses from nine transgender athletes. Three identified as men, three as women, and four as nonbinary or genderqueer (one IDed as trans male and nonbinary). They asked them a bunch of questions, got their responses, and pulled together some interesting quotes. Quotes are below, here are the themes.

Most of the people interviewed (5 of 9) didn’t believe that their ATs had knowledge about the needs of transgender athletes. They also commented on the approachability of the clinicians they interacted with, saying that most didn’t seem approachable, but some easy fixes would have been to allow more than just Male or Female for the gender section on a form or a small LGBT flag somewhere in the office. Lastly, participants roundly agreed that privacy was paramount, as they were not comfortable potentially being outed as trans by an AT misgendering them or bringing it up in casual conversation.

Tell Me More -This paper has way more than I can easily summarize and the last four pages are definitely worth a read. This isn’t a place to talk about how to fix all these problems either, though the paper did have some ideas. Instead, I’ll pop in some quotes to entice you to read it yourself. I know it’s a small study and I know these are ATs, not PTs, but do keep an open mind on this, as trans people have a really difficult time across the healthcare spectrum.

Here’s what they had to say:

“The athletic trainers I have interacted with have all seemed ignorant. They didn’t all seem unaccepting in any way, but just ignorant, is an accurate term for that; had no idea how to interact with me as a trans athlete.”

“I guess because I felt so strongly judged by my, like, perceived gender as a woman, that I couldn’t even fathom, like, the idea that the trainer could have understood a queer gender identity.’’

“It’d be a lot of pauses He would misgender me constantly. I would have to, like, remind him, ‘Hey, remember, it’s “he.”’ And he would be like ‘Oh, yeah, yeah’ and then not really say anything about it; he would just kind of blow it off.”

“I appreciate when health care professionals are receptive to the information that I’m giving them. They don’t try to fight or argue like ‘No, that’s made up’ or ‘That’s just how kids these days are.’ Or whatever. Like, they actually want to take the time to understand what being transgender is and how to better serve transgender patients.”

Check out the last four pages for more. I highly recommend them.
